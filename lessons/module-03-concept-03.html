MODULE 03 · CONCEPT 03

Knowledge cutoffs, private knowledge,
and frequently changing information

Concept 02 proved one general argument: a plain LLM's forward pass has no read channel to anything outside its frozen weights, so it can't supply knowledge that was never in its training corpus. That argument is airtight, but it's still abstract — it doesn't yet tell you which knowledge, in the field, is actually going to trip it. This concept names three independently-failable categories where the "can't" bites: knowledge cutoffs (anything dated after the corpus was collected), private or proprietary knowledge (anything never in any public corpus, regardless of age), and frequently-changing information (facts true at training time that have since moved). A single fact can trip one of these, two, or all three at once — and telling them apart is a prerequisite skill for picking the right fix later, not busywork.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Three names for when the "can't" bites

Concept 02 already argued the general case: a plain LLM can't supply a fact that was never in its training corpus. This concept isn't a second attempt at that argument — it's a taxonomy of when it fires. Three categories, each independently able to trigger it on its own:

1 · KNOWLEDGE CUTOFFS

Anything dated after the training corpus was collected. It might be fully public today — it just wasn't public yet when the snapshot was taken.

2 · PRIVATE / PROPRIETARY KNOWLEDGE

Anything never in any public training corpus, regardless of how old it is. Internal company documents are the standing example.

3 · FREQUENTLY-CHANGING INFORMATION

Facts that were true and correctly captured at training time, but have since changed. Distinct from a cutoff because even retraining on a schedule can't keep pace with how often the real value moves.

Say this plainly: a single fact can trip one of these, two of them, or all three at once. They aren't three flavors of the same failure lined up on a shelf — they're three separate gates, and a fact only has to fail one to be unreachable.

First memory: cutoff, private, and frequently-changing are three independently-failable categories, not three names for one thing — a fact can trip one, two, or all three at the same time.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

The "why" was settled. The "which one" wasn't.

It might seem like busywork to name three categories once Concept 02 has already nailed down the single root cause underneath all of them. It isn't. Each category implies a different real-world consequence once this course reaches fix-selection in Concepts 07 and 08 — a private-data problem is fixed by a connector and an authorization policy, a frequently-changing problem is fixed by continuous re-indexing, and neither is fixed by simply retraining more often. Diagnosing which category (or which combination) a failing query belongs to is a prerequisite skill for choosing the right fix, not an academic exercise in labeling.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Three Oracle RAG queries, one category each

Three questions someone might ask an Oracle RAG assistant, each failing for a mechanically different reason:

(A) · CUTOFF

"What's in the engineering runbook doc uploaded last week?" No foundation model's training window reaches next week — this document postdates any corpus collection date by construction, no matter how public it eventually becomes.

(B) · PRIVATE

"What's our internal engineering incident-response escalation policy?" Internal engineering documents were never posted anywhere public, so they were never a candidate for any training corpus — this one could be five years old and still fail the exact same way.

(C) · FREQUENTLY-CHANGING

"Who's on the payments team's on-call rotation this week?" True today, false in a few days, with no retraining cadence realistic enough to track it. Whoever the model "remembers" being on call is only ever a snapshot of one week among many.

Same symptom on the surface — a wrong or unverifiable answer — three distinct reasons underneath.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Zero terms, twice, then one new wrinkle: stale terms

Concept 02's argument was f-not-in-C: if a fact never appeared in the training corpus, it contributed zero terms to training, so nothing pushed the weights toward it. Each category reconnects to that argument for a mechanically distinct real-world reason:

CUTOFF → ZERO TERMS

The document didn't exist yet when the corpus was collected. It couldn't have contributed training terms — not "didn't," couldn't, because it wasn't there to contribute.

PRIVATE → ZERO TERMS

The document existed the whole time, but was never public, so it was never even a candidate for any corpus. Age is irrelevant; publicness is the gate.

FREQUENTLY-CHANGING → STALE TERMS

The fact was in the corpus and correct when collected. But the world's true value has since moved while the frozen weights haven't. This is the one genuinely new mechanical case this concept adds — call it "stale-terms," not "zero-terms."

05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

One shared boundary, three different relationships to it

Concept 02 already drew the closed LLM loop — frozen weights in, nothing external ever read, decode loop out. Every one of this concept's three categories sits on top of that exact same loop; what changes is only where each one lands relative to the training corpus's collection boundary, T_cutoff. Cutoff facts land just past it. Private facts never land on that axis at all, no matter the date. Frequently-changing facts land on it correctly once, then quietly split into two different values as time moves on without the weights moving too.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Retraining more often solves frequently-changing information."

No — any fixed retraining cadence is still discrete, and a discrete cadence can't structurally track a continuously-changing fact. This is only a preview line; the full rate argument comes later in the advanced section's math.

"An old document must be inside the training cutoff."

No — private documents can predate the cutoff by years and still never have been seen, because publicness is the gate, not age. Old and private are independent properties.

"These three are really just one problem."

No — same root cause per Concept 02, but different fix implications, addressed directly in Concepts 07 and 08. Lumping them together hides which fix you actually need.

08 · YOUR PROJECT MAPPING

All three, mapped onto Oracle RAG

PRIVATEInternal engineering documentation is the flagship case

Design docs, runbooks, and incident postmortems were never posted anywhere public — no amount of retraining or clever prompting reaches them, because publicness was never satisfied in the first place.

CUTOFFA schema or policy change merged this morning

Even once it becomes fully public, it postdates any foundation model's training snapshot the moment it lands — a completely different route to the same wall.

FREQUENTLY-CHANGINGCurrent sprint, on-call, and open-incident status

State this plainly: this is exactly why Oracle RAG needs a retriever pointed at live, internal, continuously-refreshed sources — not a smarter prompt, and not a retrain-more-often schedule either.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

Three named categories

Knowledge cutoff, private/proprietary knowledge, and frequently-changing information — each with a one-line definition you can state on demand.

Private ≠ cutoff, even when overlapping

A fact that is both old and never-public is still private, not cutoff — publicness is the gate that fails, not recency.

Frequently-changing ≠ cutoff

Frequently-changing is mechanically distinct: nonzero training terms that have gone stale, not zero terms from arriving too late.

Independently-failable, not mutually exclusive

A single fact can trip one, two, or all three categories at once — overlap is normal, not an edge case.

Teach-back questions

  1. Name all three categories, each with a one-line definition.
  2. Explain why private and cutoff differ even when a fact is both old and never-public.
  3. Explain why frequently-changing is mechanically distinct from cutoff, not just a variant of it.
  4. Give a worked example of a fact that trips two categories at once, and say why that's normal rather than a contradiction.

04
INTUITION

A date-stamped textbook, three different reasons it fails you

Extend Concept 02's handcuffed-exam-taker analogy with one more detail: the textbook this student was handed is date-stamped. It was printed on a fixed date — anything that happened afterward simply isn't in it, no matter how thoroughly it was studied. Some material was never given to this student at all, because it was confidential to a different class entirely — printing date is irrelevant, it was never going to be handed over. And some material describes a school rule that has since been amended, quietly, without anyone reprinting the book — the student can recite the old rule fluently and confidently, and be wrong every time. Three distinct reasons the same closed-book student can't answer "what's true right now" — not one blurry excuse.

Analogy boundaryThe student, unlike the model, would eventually notice the book felt outdated. The model has no such awareness — this is a structural property of frozen weights and a bounded corpus, not a motivational gap. Lazaridou et al.'s temporal-generalization work is the paper-level version of this same point: language models measurably degrade the further a query drifts past their training window, exactly the "date-stamped textbook" problem stated formally.

06
MECHANISM

Zero-terms, twice, then the new case: stale-terms

Trace one instance of each category through Concept 02's f∉C argument:

Cutoff: f postdates T_cutoff ⇒ f ∉ C ⇒ 0 training terms
Private: f ∉ C_public for every public C, regardless of T_cutoff ⇒ 0 training terms
Frequently-changing: f ∈ C at T_cutoff (nonzero terms) — but f_true(t) ≠ f_true(T_cutoff) for t > T_cutoff ⇒ "stale-terms," not "zero-terms"

Cutoff and private both land on zero training terms, for different real-world reasons — one arrived too late, one was never a candidate at all. Frequently-changing is the one new mechanical case this concept adds: f was in C, correctly, at collection time. The terms exist. They just support a value the real world has since walked away from.

09
MATHEMATICS

Formalizing the stale-terms drift

Define the drift of a frequently-changing fact past T_cutoff:

drift(t) = f_true(t) − f_true(T_cutoff)

θ was optimized against f_true(T_cutoff) only — nothing in training ever saw f_true(t) for t > T_cutoff. Let r_fact be the real-world rate at which the fact changes, and r_retrain be the retraining cadence. The failure condition:

r_fact > r_retrain, for some t between retraining cycles

This is precisely why b07's misconception — "just retrain more often" — reduces the size of the gap without structurally eliminating it. Any fixed r_retrain still leaves a window where drift(t) ≠ 0 for at least part of every cycle, unless r_fact happens to be zero, which is exactly the case where the fact wasn't frequently-changing to begin with. Every symbol here — θ, C, T_cutoff — ties directly back to Concept 02's original notation; only drift(t), r_fact, and r_retrain are new. Dhingra et al.'s TempLAMA framing — a dataset built explicitly around facts whose correct answer changes over time — is the closest published anchor for this exact rate argument.

10
WORKED EXAMPLE

90-day retraining against a 7-day rotation

Put numbers on it. Retraining every 90 days gives r_retrain = 1/90 per day. An on-call rotation that changes every 7 days gives r_fact = 1/7 per day — roughly 12 to 13 times more frequent than the retraining cycle. That means, between any two retrains, there are on the order of 12-13 separate stale windows of up to 7 days each where the baked-in on-call name and the real one simply don't match.

Day 0 — retrain (fresh)→ Days 1-7 — stale window 1→ Days 8-14 — stale window 2→ Days 15-21 — stale window 3→ … roughly 9 more 7-day stale windows …→ Day 90 — retrain again (fresh, briefly)

Even at the expense of a full retraining run every 90 days, the fact is wrong for most of the interval between cycles — purely from lag, before accounting for anything else going wrong. Frequently-changing information is not a "retrain harder" problem; it's a rate problem, and the rate is against you.

12
CODE

classify_knowledge_gap: a set, not a single label

PYTHON · FROM-SCRATCH PSEUDOCODE 
class Fact:
    # a tiny stand-in record -- real usage would probably be a
    # dataclass. It only needs a name and the value that was true
    # when the corpus was collected, so the classifier below can
    # detect whether the world has since drifted away from it.
    def __init__(self, name, value_at_collection):
        self.name = name
        self.value_at_collection = value_at_collection


def classify_knowledge_gap(
    fact,               # a Fact record, see above
    corpus_public,      # was this fact's *source* ever in a public corpus at all?
    collection_date,    # T_cutoff for the model being asked
    current_date,       # "now"
    last_public_seen,   # date this fact's current value went public, or None
    current_true_value, # the fact's real-world value right now
):
    # never a single label -- the three categories are
    # independently-failable, so this returns a SET that can hold
    # one, two, or all three at once (s05's last row).
    triggered = set()

    # s05 / private: never published in ANY public corpus, regardless
    # of date. Publicness is the gate -- this check doesn't even look
    # at collection_date.
    if not corpus_public:
        triggered.add("private")

    # s05 / cutoff: f postdates T_cutoff by construction. last_public_seen
    # is None when this value has no public record yet at all; it's a
    # real date once the fact actually went public.
    if corpus_public and (last_public_seen is None or last_public_seen > collection_date):
        triggered.add("cutoff")

    # s05 / frequently-changing: f WAS in C -- last_public_seen is on
    # or before collection_date, so some version of this fact had
    # nonzero training terms -- but the real value has moved since,
    # so what's baked into theta no longer equals current_true_value.
    # This is s06's "stale-terms," not "zero-terms."
    if (
        corpus_public
        and last_public_seen is not None
        and last_public_seen <= collection_date
        and fact.value_at_collection != current_true_value
    ):
        triggered.add("frequently-changing")

    return triggered


# the three Oracle RAG examples from b03/s10, run through the classifier
runbook = Fact("engineering runbook doc", value_at_collection=None)
print(classify_knowledge_gap(
    runbook, corpus_public=True, collection_date="2026-06-01",
    current_date="2026-08-22", last_public_seen="2026-08-15",
    current_true_value="v3 of the runbook",
))
# {'cutoff'} -- uploaded last week, after T_cutoff

escalation_policy = Fact("incident-response escalation policy", value_at_collection="internal-only")
print(classify_knowledge_gap(
    escalation_policy, corpus_public=False, collection_date="2026-06-01",
    current_date="2026-08-22", last_public_seen=None,
    current_true_value="internal-only",
))
# {'private'} -- never public at any date, so cutoff never even applies

on_call = Fact("payments team on-call", value_at_collection="Alex")
print(classify_knowledge_gap(
    on_call, corpus_public=True, collection_date="2026-06-01",
    current_date="2026-08-22", last_public_seen="2026-05-01",
    current_true_value="Priya",
))
# {'frequently-changing'} -- captured fine at collection time, stale now

weekly_private_policy = Fact("regional pricing exception list", value_at_collection="Tier A")
print(classify_knowledge_gap(
    weekly_private_policy, corpus_public=False, collection_date="2026-06-01",
    current_date="2026-08-22", last_public_seen=None,
    current_true_value="Tier C",
))
# {'private', 'frequently-changing'} -- s11's deliberate overlap case

Notice the last call returns two categories at once, and that this is the intended behavior, not a bug — the function's whole job is to refuse collapsing "independently-failable" into "one label per fact."

13
ECOSYSTEM

Production systems solve each category with a different piece

Production RAG systems operationalize this taxonomy as three separate engineering concerns, not one. Cutoff is handled by the initial ingestion and index build (Modules 06-10) — a single pass suffices, since cutoff-only content is static once captured. Private is handled by connector access and authorization — Module 06's source inventory decides what gets ingested at all, and Module 20's authorization-aware retrieval (forward-referenced, not detailed here) decides who can see it once it is. Frequently-changing is handled by incremental, event-driven ingestion and explicit freshness SLOs — Module 06's incremental-ingestion concept and Module 19's freshness SLOs (both forward-referenced) exist specifically because a single ingestion pass is not enough here. Gao et al.'s RAG survey frames private and enterprise data access as one of the primary real-world motivations for retrieval-augmented systems in the first place; Dhingra et al.'s TempLAMA framing is the closest published anchor for the frequently-changing category specifically.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Three failure modes specific to misdiagnosing the category

CUTOFF CREEP

Treating one ingestion pass as permanently fresh

An index built once looks solved forever, but new cutoff-type facts keep arriving on their own schedule — "indexed once" isn't the same as "current."

PRIVATE-DATA LEAKAGE MISDIAGNOSIS

Mistaking zero-density private data for sparse coverage

A wrong answer about private data can look exactly like Module 02 Concept 09's sparse-coverage hallucination. It isn't — it's zero-density private data, and it needs a completely different fix: a connector, not more examples.

FRESHNESS BLIND SPOT

"Correct at index time" quietly becomes "solved forever"

A frequently-changing fact that was right when indexed gets treated as done, with no re-index schedule or explicit freshness policy ever attached to it.

REALITY

Production implications, at introductory depth

  • Freshness SLOs need to be defined per data source, not globally — a company wiki and an on-call schedule do not share one freshness policy (Module 19, forward-referenced).
  • Private-data ingestion carries authorization and governance stakes that go well beyond mere staleness — access control matters as much as freshness (Module 20, forward-referenced).
  • Cutoff-only content is the cheapest tier to solve — one ingestion pass, and it's done.
  • Frequently-changing content is usually the most operationally expensive tier, precisely because s09's rate inequality never fully resolves on any fixed schedule. Gao et al.'s enterprise-governance framing applies directly here, too.

Knowledge check

Distinguish private from cutoff using a concrete example.

  4. "What's in the confidential legal settlement signed three years ago?"
  5. "What's the capital of France?"

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
(1) Private — an unpublished internal memo was never a candidate for any public corpus, regardless of how recent it is. (2) Cutoff — the change is presumably public-facing eventually, it just postdates any training snapshot by construction. (3) Frequently-changing — the exchange rate was correct at some point and has since moved; no fixed retraining cadence tracks a weekly rate. (4) Private — three years old and still never public; age doesn't matter, publicness does. (5) None of the three — a stable, dense, public fact, exactly the "good fit" case from s11 where a plain LLM is fine. Teach-back: in your own words, explain why lumping all three into one undifferentiated "the model doesn't know things" bucket would make it harder to design the right fix later — name at least one fix that would be wrongly applied to the wrong category if the taxonomy were collapsed.

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

The three categories

  • Cutoff: zero terms, arrived too late — one fixed boundary
  • Private: zero terms, never public at any date — publicness is the gate
  • Frequently-changing: stale terms, correct once, then drifted — r_fact > r_retrain

Oracle RAG trio

Runbook doc = cutoff. Incident-response escalation policy = private. On-call rotation = frequently-changing. Memorable, and independently-failable — a single fact can trip more than one.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Explain to a stakeholder why "we fine-tuned last quarter" doesn't fix the on-call-rotation problem.
Fine-tuning bakes in whatever was true at the moment of that training run. An on-call rotation changes weekly, so within days the fine-tuned model is already answering from a stale snapshot — the fix has to be re-applied constantly to stay current, which is exactly what makes retraining the wrong tool for anything that changes faster than the retraining schedule.
Design question: given a single wrong-answer log line, how would you determine which category caused it?
Check whether the source document was ever public — if not, it's private, regardless of the date. If it was public, compare the document's publish date to the model's training cutoff — if it postdates the cutoff, it's a cutoff failure. If the document predates the cutoff and was captured correctly, but the real-world value has since changed, it's frequently-changing. Any two of these can be true at once.
Coding: sketch the branching logic behind classify_knowledge_gap.
Three independent if-checks, each adding to a set rather than returning early: not corpus_public adds "private"; corpus_public and (no public record yet, or the public record postdates collection) adds "cutoff"; corpus_public and captured on or before collection but the value has since diverged adds "frequently-changing." Returning a set, not a single label, is the whole point — see s12.
Production scenario: a newly-acquired company's internal wiki gets ingested. Which category did it start in, and does that change once it's indexed?
Before ingestion it's purely private — never public, regardless of age. Once it's ingested into your own retrieval index, it stops being a knowledge-gap problem at all, because it's no longer being answered from frozen weights — it's being retrieved live. The category label describes the gap in parametric knowledge, not a permanent property of the document.
