MODULE 07 · CONCEPT 02

Cut it somewhere.
Just not through the middle of a word.

Concept 01 proved a document has to be split before it can be embedded safely — it never said how. This concept gives the first two answers, both purely mechanical: fixed-size chunking slices every N characters with no regard for content at all; recursive chunking prefers a natural separator first, and only falls back to a raw cut where nothing better is available. The same 146-character passage, split both ways at the same size, shows exactly what that difference buys.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Fixed-size cuts blindly; recursive looks for a seam first

THIS CONCEPT

Fixed-size chunking — slice every N characters or tokens, ignoring content entirely. Recursive chunking — try a preferred separator first (paragraph break, then line break, then space), and only fall back to a raw character cut for a piece still too large after that.

NOT THIS CONCEPT

Sentence/paragraph-aware NLP segmentation (Concept 03), semantic and proposition-based chunking (Concept 04), the chunk-size trade-off itself (Concept 05), and chunk overlap (Concept 06) — all named forward only.

First memory: both methods are purely mechanical — recursive is not "smarter" about language, it just prefers separator characters over raw ones. Understanding an actual sentence boundary is Concept 03's job.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 01 proved a split is needed. It never said which one.

Concept 01's own closing line named both methods by title without teaching either: "Concept 02 opens the actual mechanism: fixed-size chunking and recursive chunking, the simplest two ways to actually produce the smaller pieces this concept has been arguing are necessary." This concept keeps that promise. Both methods are content-agnostic — neither reads a sentence or a paragraph as a linguistic unit — which is exactly why they're the simplest possible starting point, not the best available method.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Two algorithms, one shared target size

MethodHow it decides where to cutFallback
Fixed-sizeEvery Nth character, no exceptionsNone needed — it never looks at content
RecursiveTry "\n\n" first; merge pieces back up to N; recurse into any oversized piece using "\n", then " "Raw characters, only for a single unbroken run still longer than N

Recursive chunking's own fallback list — paragraph break, then line break, then space, then raw characters — is LangChain's actual default separator order for its RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter, confirmed directly from its source. It only reaches the last, riskiest level when a single word or number run is itself longer than the chunk size — which never happens in this passage, since its longest word is 9 characters.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Recursive chunking understands sentences."

False — it only recognizes separator characters. It happens to often land near sentence-like boundaries because spaces and paragraph breaks correlate with them, not because it parses grammar.

"A bigger chunk_size always means fewer mid-word cuts for fixed-size."

False, and directly checkable: Section 07's chart shows the cut count bouncing between 0 and 4 as N increases from 20 to 80 — it is not monotonic, because it only depends on where N happens to land relative to word boundaries.

"Recursive chunking never cuts mid-word."

Only true until a single unbroken run of text (a very long word, a URL, a hash) exceeds chunk_size — at that point it falls back to the exact same raw-character cut fixed-size always uses.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

Both methods are purely mechanical

Neither fixed-size nor recursive chunking reads a sentence or paragraph as a linguistic unit — that starts at Concept 03.

Fixed-size's safety is coincidental

Whether a given chunk_size happens to avoid cutting mid-word depends only on arithmetic, not on anything about the method itself.

Recursive's safety has a real reason, and a real limit

It's safe here because no word exceeds the chunk size — it would fall back to a raw cut the moment one did.

04
INTUITION

Blind scissors vs. someone looking for a seam

Fixed-size chunking is a pair of scissors on a timer, cutting a ribbon at a fixed interval with eyes closed — it doesn't matter what's printed on the ribbon at that point. Recursive chunking is someone who first looks for an existing seam or perforation near the target length and cuts there instead; only when no seam is close enough do they close their eyes and cut blind, exactly like the first method.

Analogy boundaryThe scissors analogy explains why one method is safer on ordinary text. It does not explain the exact merge-and-recurse algorithm or the boundary-cut arithmetic — Sections 05 and 06 give those formally.

06
MECHANISM

Two lanes: no preference at all, or a fallback ladder

FIXED-SIZE LANE

Slice the text every N characters, from position 0. No merging, no separators, no recursion — the algorithm never inspects content.

RECURSIVE LANE

Split on the first separator in the list; merge consecutive pieces back together (re-inserting the separator) as long as the result stays within N; recurse into any single piece still too large using the next separator down the list.

09
MATHEMATICS

A closed form for one method, an algorithm for the other

fixed_chunk_count = ⌈docLen / N⌉
boundary_cut(i) = isWordChar(text[i·N − 1]) ∧ isWordChar(text[i·N])
recursive_chunk_count = no closed form — depends on where separators fall

Fixed-size chunking has a clean closed-form chunk count and a simple per-boundary cut test. Recursive chunking does not — its chunk count and cut behavior depend entirely on where the passage's own separators happen to fall, which is exactly why this course teaches it as an algorithm to trace, not a formula to evaluate.

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean fit, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

Fixed-width log lines or CSV rows

Content with a genuinely uniform structure and short fields — fixed-size chunking is trivially safe because there's rarely a long word to cut through in the first place.

BOUNDARY CASE

A passage containing one long URL or hash

If a single unbroken run of characters exceeds chunk_size, recursive falls all the way back to a raw cut too — its safety advantage disappears exactly there.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Assuming recursive chunking is content-aware

It only prefers separator characters. Concept 03's actual sentence and paragraph segmentation is a different, more capable mechanism, not a bigger version of this one.

12
CODE

Both methods, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
def fixed_size_chunks(text, size):
    return [text[i:i + size] for i in range(0, len(text), size)]

def is_word_char(ch):
    return ch is not None and (ch.isalnum())

def recursive_chunks(text, size, separators):
    if len(text) <= size:
        return [text]
    if not separators:
        return [text[i:i + size] for i in range(0, len(text), size)]
    sep, rest = separators[0], separators[1:]
    parts = text.split(sep)
    merged, cur = [], ""
    for part in parts:
        if len(part) > size:
            if cur:
                merged.append(cur)
                cur = ""
            merged.extend(recursive_chunks(part, size, rest))
            continue
        candidate = part if cur == "" else cur + sep + part
        if len(candidate) <= size:
            cur = candidate
        else:
            merged.append(cur)
            cur = part
    if cur:
        merged.append(cur)
    return merged

PASSAGE = ("Refund requests must be submitted within 45 days of purchase.\n\n"
           "Employees on the premium plan receive an extended 90 day window for filing a claim.")

fixed = fixed_size_chunks(PASSAGE, 42)
recursive = recursive_chunks(PASSAGE, 42, ["\n\n", "\n", " ", ""])

assert [len(c) for c in fixed] == [42, 42, 42, 20]
assert [len(c) for c in recursive] == [40, 20, 40, 42]
print("fixed:", fixed)
print("recursive:", recursive)

ECOSYSTEM

The same mechanism, in real tooling

LangChain's CharacterTextSplitter implements fixed-size chunking directly, and its RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter implements the recursive method taught here — its actual default separator list, confirmed by reading its source directly, is exactly ["\n\n", "\n", " ", ""], the same order used throughout this concept. One honest simplification: this lesson's from-scratch code drops the separator character at each chunk boundary for clarity, while LangChain's real default (keep_separator=True) keeps it, usually at the start of the following chunk — a difference worth knowing before treating this code as a literal drop-in replacement.

CONTRAST

Fixed-size vs. recursive

MethodStrengthWeakness
Fixed-sizeSimplest possible implementation — no separator logic at all, works on any byte streamMid-word cut risk depends entirely on arithmetic coincidence, not on anything controllable
RecursivePrefers natural breaks, so it's safe on ordinary prose at negligible extra costOnly as safe as its separator list for the actual content — a separator-free run still forces a raw cut

Decision framework: default to recursive for ordinary prose — it's almost free and meaningfully safer. Reach for fixed-size only when content genuinely has no useful separators (fixed-width records, some binary-adjacent formats) where recursive's extra logic buys nothing.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

SILENT ARITHMETIC LUCK

Fixed-size "working" on a test passage by accident

A chunk_size that happens to avoid mid-word cuts on one sample document offers no guarantee on the next one — the safety was never designed, just coincidental.

UNEXPECTED RAW-CUT FALLBACK

Recursive silently degrading to fixed-size behavior

A single long token (URL, hash, unbroken identifier) exceeding chunk_size forces the exact same raw cut fixed-size always uses, with no warning.

SEPARATOR MISMATCH

A separator list tuned for prose, applied to code or logs

The default ["\n\n","\n"," ",""] list assumes prose-like structure — content with different natural breaks (e.g. code) may need a different separator list entirely, named forward to production tuning.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Real pipelines default to recursive over plain fixed-size at near-identical implementation cost, precisely because the safety margin is close to free.
  • chunk_size in characters must still map to something reasonable once tokenized, tying back to Concept 01's embedding-model token cap — a character count and a token count are not interchangeable.
  • Neither method here is content-aware; Concepts 03 and 04 are the actual production-grade upgrades once mechanical splitting stops being good enough.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why does recursive chunking avoid the exact two cuts fixed-size makes in the worked example?
Both of recursive's problem boundaries (around "45" and "premium") sit at a space or paragraph break in the original text. Recursive prefers to split exactly there, dropping the separator instead of cutting through the surrounding word.
Under what condition does recursive chunking fall back to a raw character cut?
Only when a single unbroken run of text (no "\n\n", "\n", or " " anywhere inside it) is itself longer than chunk_size — at that point there's no separator left to prefer, and it slices arithmetically, exactly like fixed-size.
Why isn't fixed-size's mid-word cut count safe to assume shrinks as chunk_size grows?
The cut count only depends on where chunk_size happens to land relative to word boundaries in that specific passage — Section 07's chart shows it bouncing between 0 and 4 with no consistent trend as size increases from 20 to 80.

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
Fixed-size at N=10: "The cat sa", "t on the s", "oft mat." — 3 chunks (10, 10, 8 chars). Both internal boundaries land inside a word: "sat"→"sa"/"t" and "soft"→"s"/"oft" — 2 of 2 cuts. PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

Fixed-size and recursive chunking

  • Fixed-size: slice every N characters, no exceptions — safety is arithmetic coincidence
  • Recursive: prefer "\n\n", then "\n", then " ", fall back to raw characters only for an oversized unbroken run
  • Core relation: recursive's safety margin disappears exactly where fixed-size's does — on a single token longer than chunk_size

Memorable example

The same 146-character passage, same chunk_size=42, same 4-chunk count: fixed-size cuts "45" and "premium" in half (2 of 3 boundaries); recursive cuts neither (0 of 3), because every one of its boundaries lands on a dropped space or paragraph break instead.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why is recursive chunking usually preferred over fixed-size in production?
It's nearly free to implement and meaningfully reduces mid-word/mid-number cuts on ordinary prose by preferring existing separators, only degrading to fixed-size's behavior on separator-free runs.
Mathematical: derive fixed-size chunking's chunk count formula.
chunk_count = ⌈docLen / chunk_size⌉ — every chunk except possibly the last is exactly chunk_size characters; the last holds the remainder.
Coding: implement the merge-and-recurse step of a recursive splitter.
Split the text on the current separator, then greedily merge consecutive parts (re-inserting the separator) while the merged length stays within chunk_size; when a single part alone exceeds chunk_size, recurse into just that part using the next separator in the list.
Debugging: a recursive splitter is producing suspiciously many small chunks. What do you check?
Whether the content contains long separator-free runs (URLs, hashes, minified code) that keep forcing raw-character fallback splits, or whether the separator list itself is mismatched to the content type (a prose-tuned list applied to code, for instance).
System design: how would you choose between fixed-size and recursive for a new corpus?
Profile the corpus for long separator-free runs. If they're rare, recursive is a strictly better default at negligible extra cost. If the content genuinely lacks useful separators throughout (fixed-width records, certain binary-adjacent formats), recursive's extra logic buys nothing over fixed-size.
