MODULE 06 · CONCEPT 05

Readable is not
the same as clean.

Concepts 02, 03 and 04 each handed forward the same three words: a raw, unclean, unchunked record. Whatever the source — a parsed file, a database row, a scanned page — getting a full, correctly-ordered record out was the job; making its content trustworthy was deliberately deferred every time. This concept is where "unclean" finally gets addressed, and for exactly three defects: encoding artifacts (mojibake, from decoding bytes under the wrong charset), boilerplate (nav, footer and legal chrome repeating verbatim across every page or record from one source), and whitespace, punctuation and residual noise — including Concept 04's own uncorrected "0RDER 447l," left exactly as it was until now. Deduplicating identical records across sources is a different problem, and it's Concept 06's, not this one's.

PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

Three defects, three fixes, one word finally dropped

Concepts 02, 03 and 04 all produced a full, correctly-ordered record and then stopped — none of them addressed whether that record's text was actually trustworthy. This concept covers exactly that: fixing encoding artifacts, removing boilerplate, and normalizing whitespace and punctuation (plus residual noise) in a record that's already extracted and already in the right order. Nothing here decides whether two records are the same record seen twice — that's Concept 06 — and nothing here decides where a chunk boundary falls — that's Module 07.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Fixing three specific defects, nothing more

CLEANING AND NORMALIZATION (THIS CONCEPT)

Fixing encoding artifacts, removing boilerplate, and normalizing whitespace and punctuation plus residual character-level noise — inside one already-extracted, correctly-ordered record.

NOT THIS CONCEPT (CONCEPT 06)

Deciding whether two or more separately-cleaned records, possibly from different sources, are actually the same underlying thing — a problem about identity across records, not noise within one.

First memory: a record can be perfectly readable and still be wrong in three completely independent ways — a decoder mismatch, a repeated block of chrome, or leftover rough edges — and fixing one says nothing about the other two.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Getting a record out was never the same job as making it trustworthy

Concept 02's parsers, Concept 03's non-file extractors, and Concept 04's OCR and layout analysis all had one job: produce a full, correctly-ordered record from whatever source they were pointed at. None of them checked whether that record was decoded under the right character encoding, none of them stripped the nav and footer chrome that a scraped or exported source repeats on every page, and none of them tidied the irregular spacing and residual noise extraction can leave behind. That was never an oversight — it was a deliberate boundary. This concept is where those three specific gaps finally get closed.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

One garbled apostrophe, one repeated nav bar, one leftover OCR mistake

ENCODING

The word "don't" contains a right single quotation mark that UTF-8-encodes as three bytes. Decoded under the wrong charset, those same three bytes render as "donâ€™t" — one intended character balloons into three garbled ones, five characters becoming seven.

BOILERPLATE

A support page's "Home | Support | Contact" nav bar gets pulled in at both the top and bottom of every single page scraped from that source — useful for navigation once, dead weight in a retrieval corpus every time after.

WHITESPACE / PUNCTUATION

Irregular spacing and stray punctuation left over from extraction — and Concept 04's own uncorrected "0RDER 447l" is exactly the kind of leftover noise this step finally addresses, rather than leaving it as OCR's problem forever.

None of these three failures announces itself as an obvious error — each one just quietly makes a lookup, a match, or a retrieved passage a little less reliable than it looks.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Cleaning is just trimming whitespace."

Whitespace is one of three independent defects. A record can have perfect spacing and still be full of mojibake or repeated boilerplate.

"If text displays fine, encoding must be correct."

Mojibake doesn't always look obviously broken — some garbled sequences render as plausible-looking text that only breaks an exact-match lookup, not a casual glance.

"Removing boilerplate once by hand is enough."

A source's template can drift after a redesign, and a rule tuned to one exact block silently stops matching a slightly-changed one.

"Cleaning finishes the job."

A fully cleaned record is still unchunked and still not checked against every other record for duplication — cleaning closes three specific gaps, not every gap.

04
INTUITION

A wrong radio frequency, a reprinted letterhead, a proofreader's second pass

Encoding artifacts are a wrong radio frequency: the signal — the bytes — never changed, but tuning in with the wrong decoder turns clear speech into static-laced noise. Boilerplate is a letterhead reprinted on every page of a fax: useful once, dead weight copied a hundred times over. Whitespace and punctuation noise is what a proofreader catches on a second pass: nothing structurally wrong, just rough edges an exact-match system can't see past.

Three repairs, not oneCleaning is not one generic step wearing three names. Fixing a decoder mismatch, stripping a repeated template, and tightening spacing are three independent operations — a record can pass one, fail the other two, and any combination in between, exactly what Section 14's lab demonstrates directly.

06
MECHANISM

Three lanes, one shared handoff

Encoding lane — detect or assume the source's real byte encoding, then repair mojibake by reversing the mis-decode: re-encode the garbled text under the charset that was wrongly assumed, then decode those bytes under the correct one (Section 12's from-scratch function does exactly this on the verified don't/donâ€™t pair).
Boilerplate lane — match and remove known repeated blocks (nav, footer, legal disclaimers) that appear verbatim across every page or record from the same source, without touching content that merely happens to repeat by design (Section 11's FAQ "Q:" mistake to avoid).
Whitespace + punctuation lane — collapse irregular spacing, standardize punctuation spacing, and apply targeted, context-driven corrections to residual character-level noise left over from extraction — including Concept 04's own uncorrected "0RDER 447l," addressed here for the first time.
Handoff — every lane still ends at the same place Concepts 02–04 ended, minus one word: a structurally-correct, now-clean record — still unchunked (Module 07) and still not deduplicated against other records (Concept 06).

The boilerplate arithmetic is simple but adds up fast: a 93-character nav/footer block appearing once before and once after a 208-character body, joined by two single-space separators, produces a 396-character raw extract (93 + 1 + 208 + 1 + 93). Stripping both copies removes 188 characters — about 47.5% of the raw extract — and leaves exactly the 208-character body.

09
MATHEMATICS

The same CER formula, reused for a different purpose

Concept 04 defined character error rate in full: given a reference string of length N and a hypothesis string, with S substitutions, D deletions and I insertions under the minimum-cost (Levenshtein) alignment between the two,

CER = (S + D + I) / N

That definition is not re-derived here — it's reused for a different purpose. Concept 04 used CER to measure how much an OCR engine's raw output diverged from the truth. This concept uses the exact same formula to measure how much a cleaning correction recovers: compute CER once against the dirty hypothesis, apply a targeted fix, then compute CER again against the corrected hypothesis. The formula doesn't change; what changes is which string sits in the "hypothesis" slot.

CONCRETE USE

Four cases: two clean fits, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

A digitally-authored ticket note with correct, consistent UTF-8 throughout

No mojibake, no repeated chrome, no irregular spacing — this concept has nothing extra to do.

CLEAN FIT

Mojibake from a known, declared mis-encoding

A system that reliably exports Windows-1252 mislabeled as UTF-8 produces mojibake that's deterministically repairable, because the exact wrong-charset assumption is already known.

BOUNDARY CASE

Mojibake that's already lossy — the original bytes are gone

Text that's already round-tripped through multiple lossy transcodings can leave permanent replacement characters where information used to be; no repair recovers bytes that no longer exist.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Over-aggressive boilerplate stripping deleting legitimate repeated content

Removing every line that starts with a repeated string like "Q:" because it looks boilerplate-like destroys legitimate structured content — an FAQ's questions — that happens to repeat by design, not by accident.

CODE

Three cleaners, from scratch

This is the mechanism behind Section 06's three lanes: a mojibake repair function tested on the verified don't/donâ€™t pair, an exact-block boilerplate stripper, and a whitespace/punctuation normalizer — plus one edge case every production cleaner needs to pass: running twice on already-clean text must not change it.

PYTHON · MOJIBAKE REPAIR, BOILERPLATE STRIPPING, WHITESPACE NORMALIZATION 
NAV = "Home | Support | Contact"

def fix_mojibake(text):
    # Reverse a wrong-charset decode by re-encoding under the charset
    # that was wrongly assumed, then decoding under the real one.
    try:
        return text.encode("cp1252").decode("utf-8")
    except (UnicodeDecodeError, UnicodeEncodeError):
        return text  # not mojibake under this hypothesis -- leave it alone

def strip_boilerplate(text):
    out = text
    if out.startswith(NAV):
        out = out[len(NAV):]
    out = out.lstrip()
    if out.endswith(NAV):
        out = out[:-len(NAV)]
    return out.rstrip()

import re

def normalize_whitespace(text):
    out = re.sub(r"\s+,and", ", and", text)
    out = out.replace("0RDER 447l", "ORDER 4471")
    out = re.sub(r" {2,}", " ", out)
    return out.strip()

mojibake = "don" + chr(0xE2) + chr(0x20AC) + chr(0x2122) + "t"  # "donâ€™t"
assert len(mojibake) == 7
fixed = fix_mojibake(mojibake)
assert fixed == "don" + chr(0x2019) + "t"                        # "don't"
assert len(fixed) == 5

# Edge case: idempotency. Running a cleaner on already-clean text
# must not change it -- Section 18's production concern, checked here.
already_clean = "don" + chr(0x2019) + "t know when it will update."
assert fix_mojibake(already_clean) == already_clean
assert normalize_whitespace(already_clean) == already_clean
assert strip_boilerplate(already_clean) == already_clean

ECOSYSTEM

The same three lanes, in real tools

Encoding: ftfy ("fixes text for you") is the standard open-source library for automated mojibake repair, applying exactly the re-encode/re-decode heuristic Section 12 builds from scratch. Whitespace and punctuation: Python's built-in unicodedata.normalize applies NFC or NFKC canonical forms per Unicode Standard Annex #15, standardizing equivalent-but-differently-encoded character sequences. Boilerplate: content-extraction heuristics in the style of trafilatura and Boilerpipe detect and strip template chrome from web pages using structural and statistical signals rather than one exact string match, named here without a separate link-verification pass, consistent with Concept 04's own precedent of naming more tools than it links. Framework-level connectors (LangChain, LlamaIndex — Modules 12 and 14) expose several of these as document transformers behind one common interface, the same file-format-versus-mechanism trade Concepts 02–04 all named already.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

WRONG-GUESS REPAIR

Applying a mojibake fix to text that was never mis-decoded

Running Section 12's re-encode/re-decode round-trip on already-correct UTF-8 text can corrupt valid characters instead of fixing anything — the round-trip has to be conditional, not automatic.

TEMPLATE DRIFT

Boilerplate leaks through after a site redesign

An exact-string stripper tuned to "Home | Support | Contact" misses "Home | Support | Contact | v2" the moment a source changes its template even slightly.

OVER-NORMALIZATION

A whitespace/punctuation pass deletes meaningful structure

Collapsing every run of spaces to one can flatten a deliberately indented code block or table-like list into unreadable prose — normalization needs a boundary, not a blanket rule.

17
CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
Cleaning is always harmless to runFalse — Section 16's wrong-guess repair and over-normalization failures both show a cleaning step can actively damage already-correct text if it's applied unconditionally.
A statistical charset detector is always rightFalse — charset detection is a best guess from limited evidence; a known-source charset assumption is safer wherever one is available, since detectors fail silently often enough to matter.
Boilerplate is just visual clutter, not a retrieval problemFalse — Section 14's lab verdict makes this explicit: boilerplate that survives into the corpus keeps consuming retrieval's limited context budget on identical chrome, every single time it's retrieved.
18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Idempotency has to be checked before a cleaner ships, not assumed — running any cleaning function twice on already-clean text must produce identical output, exactly Section 12's edge case.
  • Boilerplate templates drift as sites redesign — a stripper that worked last quarter can silently start missing a source's new nav markup, so template rules need periodic revalidation, not one-time setup.
  • Cleaning-rule versioning matters for Concept 06's dedup hashing — if two records were cleaned by different rule versions, a hash-based duplicate check can miss a match that a consistent cleaning pass would have caught.
  • NFC vs. NFKC is a real decision with downstream consequences, not a default to pick silently — NFKC folds more aggressively (e.g. collapsing typographic variants), which helps matching but can also erase distinctions a corpus actually needs.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why does mojibake happen if none of the original bytes are ever lost?
Because the underlying byte sequence for the original character is unchanged — mojibake comes from decoding those same bytes under the wrong charset assumption, not from losing any bytes. A decoder that assumes a different charset than the one used to encode the text reads the same bytes through the wrong table and produces different characters entirely.
Why can't boilerplate removal use one universal rule across every source?
Because each source's nav, footer and legal template is different text. A rule tuned to one source's exact repeated block won't match a different source's template, and even the same source's template can drift after a redesign.
Why is over-normalization also a failure, not just "less aggressive cleaning"?
Because normalization can delete or distort meaningful content — legitimate repeated structure, deliberate spacing — that isn't noise at all. That's a different kind of damage than under-cleaning, not merely an incomplete fix.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

How would you detect mojibake programmatically, without a human scanning every record?
Attempt the re-encode/re-decode round-trip (encode under a suspected wrong charset, decode under the real one) and check whether the result contains fewer non-ASCII "junk" characters or matches expected-language statistics better than the original — a successful, clean round-trip is itself evidence the text was mojibake.
A boilerplate stripper that worked fine for months suddenly starts leaving nav chrome in the corpus. What's the likely cause, and how do you fix it?
The source most likely redesigned its template, so the exact-match (or near-match) rule no longer fires. The fix is revalidating the stripper's known-block pattern against the source's current markup, not assuming a one-time rule stays correct forever.
How would you code a known-block boilerplate stripper that's safe against deleting legitimate repeated content?
Match the exact known block (or a tightly scoped template pattern for it) at the specific positions it's expected to appear — typically a document's start and end — rather than deleting any line that merely resembles the block anywhere in the body, which is what risks catching legitimate repeated content like FAQ prefixes.
You're normalizing a multilingual corpus. How do you decide between NFC and NFKC?
NFC preserves more typographic distinctions and is usually safer as a default; NFKC folds compatibility variants more aggressively, which helps exact-match retrieval but can erase distinctions (like certain typographic or width variants) that matter for some languages or domains — the choice has to be made deliberately per corpus, not left as a silent default.
