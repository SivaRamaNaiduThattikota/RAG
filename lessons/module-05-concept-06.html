MODULE 05 · CONCEPT 06

Let repetition
run out of steam.

Concept 05 multiplied tf and idf together and, on its way out, named exactly two things it left broken: raw tf grows without any ceiling, and nothing in the formula adjusts for document length. This concept fixes the first one completely. BM25 keeps Concept 04's idf untouched and replaces the raw tf factor with a saturating version, controlled by a parameter k1, so that a term's tenth occurrence adds far less than its second did. The full BM25 formula also contains a second parameter, b, that adjusts for document length — this concept writes it into the formula and uses it, but every worked example here deliberately holds document length equal to the corpus average, so b has no visible effect yet. Concept 07 removes that simplification and derives, with real numbers, why b is there at all.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Same idf, a smarter tf, one new knob

BEFORE — CONCEPT 05

tf-idf(t, d) = tf(t, d) × idf(t). Raw tf, multiplied straight through — no limit on how large it can get.

NOW — CONCEPT 06

BM25(t, d) = idf(t) × [ tf(t,d) × (k1+1) ] / [ tf(t,d) + k1 × L(d) ]. idf is untouched; tf is replaced by a fraction that saturates toward k1+1 as tf grows; L(d) is a length term this concept holds at exactly 1 for every document.

First memory: BM25 doesn't touch idf at all — it only replaces how tf is used, and adds two dials, k1 and b, to control that replacement.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

A term's twentieth occurrence isn't worth twenty times its first

Concept 05's Section 07 already named the problem in passing: raw tf-idf lets a term repeated 50 times contribute 50 times the weight of appearing once, with no ceiling anywhere. In practice, that's a bad model of relevance — once a document has used a term five or six times, you already know it's on-topic for that term; a seventh, eighth, or fiftieth repetition tells you almost nothing new, and may just mean the document is long or repetitive. BM25 exists to make the score behave that way: rewarding the first few occurrences strongly, then flattening out.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

"Refund" in ticket T3, both ways

Reusing Concept 05's five-ticket corpus and idf("refund") ≈ 0.9163, and holding L(d) = 1 for now (this concept's simplification):

RAW TF-IDF (CONCEPT 05)

tf("refund", T3) = 2 → score = 2 × 0.9163 ≈ 1.8326. If T3 had repeated "refund" 10 times instead, the score would be 10 × 0.9163 ≈ 9.163 — five times larger, in direct proportion.

BM25, k1 = 1.2

tf = 2 → saturating factor = 2×(1.2+1) / (2+1.2×1) = 4.4/3.2 = 1.375 → BM25 score = 1.375 × 0.9163 ≈ 1.2599. If tf were 10 instead, the factor only reaches 1.9643 → BM25 score ≈ 1.7999 — nowhere near five times larger.

Raw tf-idf treats occurrence 10 as worth exactly as much as occurrence 1. BM25 treats occurrence 10 as worth a small fraction of what occurrence 1 was worth, because the ratio is already most of the way to its ceiling.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

The full formula, with one term held fixed on purpose

FULL FORMULA

BM25(t, d) = idf(t) × [ tf(t,d) × (k1+1) ] / [ tf(t,d) + k1 × L(d) ], where L(d) = (1 − b + b × dl / avgdl), dl is document d's length, and avgdl is the corpus's average document length.

WHAT THIS CONCEPT HOLDS FIXED

Every worked example in this concept assumes dl = avgdl for the document in question, which makes L(d) = 1 − b + b × 1 = 1 exactly, regardless of what b is set to. That collapses the denominator to tf(t,d) + k1, isolating k1's saturation effect with nothing from b visible yet.

WHAT'S STILL NOT HAPPENING

b is a real parameter of a real formula being used correctly here — it just has nothing to do while L(d) = 1. Concept 07 varies document length for real and shows exactly what b changes.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"A smaller k1 means less saturation."

Backwards. A smaller k1 saturates faster — with k1 = 0, the factor equals 1 for any tf ≥ 1 immediately, as if the term were just present or absent. A larger k1 lets tf matter over a wider range before flattening, behaving more like raw tf-idf.

"BM25 has no upper bound, like raw tf-idf."

It does, per term: the saturating factor can never exceed k1+1, no matter how large tf grows. Section 09 proves this algebraically.

"Since b appears in the formula, it must be doing something in every example."

Not in this concept's examples — every worked case here assumes dl = avgdl, which forces L(d) = 1 regardless of b's value. b only has a visible effect once document lengths actually differ, which is Concept 07's job.

"BM25 replaces idf too."

It doesn't — idf(t) in this concept's formula is exactly Concept 04's idf, unchanged. Only the tf factor is replaced.

Modern Oracle Text scoring (Module 10, Concept 07, forward reference) and most production lexical engines use a BM25-family formula specifically because of the saturation this concept derives — a support ticket that says "refund" fifty times should not automatically outrank one that says it three times.

04
INTUITION

A judge who stops being impressed after the third repetition

Imagine the same judge from Concept 05, now scoring how many times a contestant mentions a keyword. The first mention earns full credit — clear evidence the contestant is on topic. The second and third mentions still earn real credit, but a little less each time. By the tenth mention, the judge is barely adjusting the score at all — the point was already made nine mentions ago, and the judge has learned not to reward pure repetition as if it were nine independent pieces of evidence.

Analogy boundaryThe judge analogy explains why saturation is reasonable, but not the specific algebraic shape BM25 uses to produce it, or why that shape has an exact ceiling of k1+1 — Section 09 derives that with algebra, not analogy.

06
MECHANISM

Replace tf, cap the ratio, sum across the query

Start from Concept 04/05's numbers — tf(t,d) and idf(t), already computed, nothing new.
Compute L(d) — L(d) = 1 − b + b×dl/avgdl. This concept sets dl = avgdl everywhere, so L(d) = 1 no matter what b is.
Saturate tf — replace tf(t,d) with tf(t,d)(k1+1) / [tf(t,d) + k1·L(d)] — a ratio that starts at 1 when tf=1 and rises toward k1+1 as tf grows.
Multiply by idf, then sum across the query — exactly Concept 05's structure, with the saturating ratio standing in for raw tf.

09
MATHEMATICS

Proving the ceiling exists

saturating ratio(tf) = tf(k1+1) / (tf + k1), holding L(d) = 1 As tf → ∞: tf(k1+1) / (tf + k1) → (k1+1) / 1 = k1+1

Divide numerator and denominator by tf: the ratio becomes (k1+1) / (1 + k1/tf). As tf grows large, k1/tf shrinks toward 0, so the whole expression approaches (k1+1)/1 = k1+1 — the ceiling. At tf = 1 exactly, the ratio is (k1+1)/(1+k1) = 1, always, regardless of k1's value — every saturating curve starts at exactly 1 no matter how k1 is tuned; only the climb toward k1+1 afterward depends on k1.

11
CONCRETE USE

Two clean fits, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

A term repeated many times by coincidence or padding

BM25's ceiling stops that repetition from dominating the score the way raw tf-idf would let it.

CLEAN FIT

A term appearing exactly once

Saturating ratio = 1 always at tf=1, so BM25 and raw tf-idf agree exactly for any term that appears exactly once — the two formulas only diverge once repetition starts.

BOUNDARY CASE

tf = 0

The saturating ratio's numerator is 0×(k1+1) = 0, so BM25(t,d) = 0 exactly, matching raw tf-idf's behavior for an absent term — both formulas agree at the other boundary too.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Tuning k1 to "fix" a length problem

k1 only controls how fast repetition saturates for a single document — it has no mechanism for comparing one document's length to another's. Reaching for k1 to fix a length-related ranking complaint is reaching for the wrong parameter; that's b's job, examined next.

12
CODE

BM25's saturating term weight, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
import math

tickets = {
    "T1": "my order is late and my order never arrived",
    "T2": "please refund my order immediately",
    "T3": "the refund was processed but the refund amount is wrong",
    "T4": "where is my order confirmation email",
    "T5": "i need help resetting my password",
}

def term_frequency(term, text):
    return text.split(" ").count(term)

def document_frequency(term, corpus):
    return sum(1 for text in corpus.values() if term in text.split(" "))

def inverse_document_frequency(term, corpus):
    df = document_frequency(term, corpus)
    if df == 0:
        return None
    return math.log(len(corpus) / df)

def bm25_term(term, doc_id, corpus, k1=1.2, b=0.75, length_term=1.0):
    # length_term defaults to 1.0 here on purpose: this concept always
    # assumes dl == avgdl, so b never changes the result. Concept 07
    # computes a real length_term and passes it in instead of 1.0.
    idf = inverse_document_frequency(term, corpus)
    if idf is None:
        return 0.0
    tf = term_frequency(term, corpus[doc_id])
    ratio = (tf * (k1 + 1)) / (tf + k1 * length_term)
    return idf * ratio

# tf=1 case: BM25 and raw tf-idf must agree exactly.
raw_tfidf_t2 = term_frequency("refund", tickets["T2"]) * inverse_document_frequency("refund", tickets)
assert abs(bm25_term("refund", "T2", tickets) - raw_tfidf_t2) < 1e-9

# tf=2 case: BM25 must score lower than raw tf-idf once repetition saturates.
bm25_t3 = bm25_term("refund", "T3", tickets)
raw_tfidf_t3 = term_frequency("refund", tickets["T3"]) * inverse_document_frequency("refund", tickets)
assert bm25_t3 < raw_tfidf_t3
assert abs(bm25_t3 - 1.2599) < 0.001

# Absent term: both formulas agree at exactly 0.
assert bm25_term("shipping", "T3", tickets) == 0.0

ECOSYSTEM

Where production systems run this exact formula

Elasticsearch and OpenSearch have used a BM25-family scoring function as their default similarity since Elasticsearch 5 (2017), with k1 defaulting to 1.2 and b defaulting to 0.75 — the same defaults used throughout this concept's examples. Apache Lucene's BM25Similarity class implements the identical formula this section derived, including the same length-adjustment term this concept has been holding at 1. Both systems expose k1 and b as tunable per-field parameters precisely because different document collections saturate and normalize differently.

DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

MISTUNED k1

k1 too low flattens genuine signal

An extremely low k1 makes every nonzero tf score nearly identical, discarding real evidence that a document discusses a term extensively versus in passing.

MISTUNED k1

k1 too high behaves like raw tf-idf again

A very large k1 barely saturates within realistic tf ranges, reintroducing much of the padding vulnerability this concept exists to fix.

STILL UNADDRESSED

Length bias survives this concept untouched

Because every example here holds dl = avgdl, a genuinely long document that racks up higher tf purely by being verbose would still be over-rewarded by this concept's formula — Concept 07 exists specifically because this gap is still open.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • k1 and b are almost always left at their library defaults (1.2 and 0.75) unless a relevance-evaluation process (Concepts 08–10, and Module 18 later) provides evidence that retuning them measurably improves ranking quality on a specific collection.
  • Computing the saturating ratio per (term, document) pair adds negligible cost over raw tf — it's a fixed handful of arithmetic operations per posting, not an extra pass over the corpus.
  • Because BM25 is stateless per query (it doesn't need to see other candidate documents to score one), it composes cleanly with the inverted-index-driven retrieval Concept 02 already built — no architecture change is required to adopt it over raw tf-idf.

RECALL

Knowledge check

Write BM25's saturating ratio and state its ceiling.
tf(k1+1) / (tf + k1·L(d)). As tf → ∞ (with L(d) fixed), the ratio approaches k1+1 and never exceeds it.
Why do BM25 and raw tf-idf produce identical scores at tf=1?
At tf=1, the saturating ratio is (1)(k1+1)/(1+k1) = 1 for any k1, so BM25(t,d) = idf(t) × 1 = tf-idf(t,d) exactly.
Why does this concept's b parameter have no visible effect on any computed score?
Every example holds document length equal to the corpus average, which forces L(d) = 1−b+b×1 = 1 regardless of b's value — b only changes the score once dl ≠ avgdl, which Concept 07 introduces.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
Saturating ratio at tf=2: 2×2.2/(2+1.2) = 4.4/3.2 = 1.375. BM25("order", T1) = 1.375 × 0.5108 ≈ 0.7024. Saturating ratio at tf=1 is always exactly 1, so BM25("order", T2 or T4) = 1 × 0.5108 = 0.5108, identical to their raw tf-idf scores. Raw tf-idf("order", T1) = 2 × 0.5108 ≈ 1.0217. BM25 rewards T1's second occurrence by only 0.7024 − 0.5108 ≈ 0.1916, versus raw tf-idf's full 1.0217 − 0.5108 ≈ 0.5109 — BM25 gives less than half the credit for that second occurrence that raw tf-idf did.

Teach-back: explain, in your own words, why BM25's "less than half credit" number for a second occurrence is not arbitrary — connect it back to k1's role in the saturating ratio.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Explain BM25 to someone who already understands tf-idf, in under thirty seconds.
BM25 keeps idf exactly as it was, but replaces raw term frequency with a version that saturates — instead of a term's twentieth occurrence being worth twenty times its first, BM25 caps how much repetition can help, controlled by a parameter k1, and also adjusts for document length through a second parameter, b.
What is the ceiling of BM25's saturating ratio, and at what tf value does it equal exactly 1?
The ceiling is k1+1, approached but never reached as tf grows. The ratio equals exactly 1 at tf=1, for any value of k1.
A teammate claims raising k1 will make BM25 handle very long, repetitive documents more fairly. Is that correct?
No — k1 only controls how fast repetition of a single term saturates within one document; it has no notion of comparing that document's length to others. The parameter that addresses length is b, examined in Concept 07.
Design question: why do BM25 and raw tf-idf produce identical rankings whenever every candidate document's tf for the query term is either 0 or 1?
Because the saturating ratio equals exactly 0 at tf=0 and exactly 1 at tf=1 regardless of k1 — with no tf value above 1 in play, BM25's score for every document is identical to its raw tf-idf score, so any ranking difference between the two formulas can only appear once some document's tf reaches 2 or more.
