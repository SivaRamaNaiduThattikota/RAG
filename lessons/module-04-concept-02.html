MODULE 04 · CONCEPT 02

Vector norms, dimensions
and normalization.

Concept 01 defined a vector as an ordered list of numbers and deliberately left two questions unanswered: how long is that list, and how big is the vector as a whole? This concept answers both. The list's length gets a name — dimension. The vector's overall size gets a number called a norm, computed two different ways: the L1 norm (add up the absolute values) and the L2 norm (the familiar straight-line length). Dividing a vector by its own norm rescales it to length one — normalization — using nothing but that one vector's own numbers. Nothing here compares two vectors to each other; that's dot products, angles, and cosine similarity, next concept.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Dimension, norm, and normalization — three new words, one vector, no comparisons

DIMENSION

The count of components in the list. (3, 4) has dimension 2 — literally, how many numbers.

NORM

A single number describing how big the whole vector is, computed from its own components — its magnitude, or length.

L1 NORM

Add up the absolute value of every component. For (3, 4): |3| + |4| = 7.

L2 NORM

Square every component, add them up, take the square root. For (3, 4): √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5 — the familiar straight-line length.

NORMALIZATION

Divide every component by the vector's own norm, so the result has norm exactly 1 — still one vector, rescaled, never two.

NOT YET: COMPARING VECTORS

Nothing here asks whether two vectors point the same way or how similar they are — that's dot products and cosine similarity, next concept.

First memory: dimension counts the numbers; a norm measures their combined size, in one of at least two different ways (L1 or L2); normalization rescales one vector to size exactly 1, using nothing but its own numbers.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 01 could describe a vector's shape, never its size

Concept 01's notation can write down any vector precisely, but it has no way to say that one vector is "bigger" than another of the same dimension, or to shrink an oversized vector down to some standard scale before anything later gets built on top of it. Two vectors can share a dimension and still differ enormously in magnitude — Concept 01's toolkit alone can't say by how much.

WITHOUT NORMS

Every vector's "size" stays a vague impression — useful for eyeballing a small example, useless for writing an equation or a threshold on top of it.

WITH NORMS

Every vector gets an exact magnitude, computed one of two well-defined ways, and can be rescaled to a standard length before anything else happens to it.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

How strong is one support ticket's keyword signal?

A support ticket mentions "refund" 3 times and "billing" 4 times. Written as a vector in that fixed order, t = (3, 4) — raw counts this time, not Concept 01's 0-to-1 scores, chosen because the arithmetic comes out clean.

DIMENSION

2 — two counts, refund then billing, in that fixed order (Concept 01's rule).

L1 NORM

|3| + |4| = 7 — walk 3 blocks one way and 4 the other, then add the blocks.

L2 NORM

√(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5 — the straight-line distance from the origin to the point (3, 4).

NORMALIZED VECTOR

(3/5, 4/5) = (0.6, 0.8) — same direction, rescaled so its own L2 norm is exactly 1.

Embedding vectors (Module 08) are commonly normalized this same way before they're ever compared to anything — why that helps is a question for later, not today. Today's job is only computing the number, not using it.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Three formulas, applied to one vector at a time

DIMENSION

Count the components

dim(v) = n, where v has n components — Concept 01's n, finally named.

L1 NORM

Sum of absolute values

‖v‖₁ = |v₁| + |v₂| + … + |vₙ| — also called the taxicab or Manhattan norm.

L2 NORM

Square root of sum of squares

‖v‖₂ = √(v₁² + v₂² + … + vₙ²) — also called the Euclidean norm.

NORMALIZATION

Divide by the L2 norm

v̂ = v / ‖v‖₂ — by default, "normalize" means rescale to L2 norm 1 unless stated otherwise.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"A vector's dimension and its norm are the same idea."

Dimension counts components; norm measures magnitude. (3, 4) and (10, 0) both have dimension 2, but L2 norms 5 and 10 — same dimension, different size.

"L1 and L2 norms always give the same number."

They only coincide when a vector has at most one non-zero component. Section 03's (3, 4) gives 7 and 5 — not the same number.

"Normalizing a vector changes which way it points."

Every component gets divided by the same positive number (the norm), so direction never changes — only magnitude shrinks to exactly 1.

"You need a second vector before you can normalize the first one."

Normalization only uses the vector's own norm. No comparison to anything else happens in this concept at all.

04
INTUITION

Walking city blocks versus flying straight

To reach a point 3 blocks east and 4 blocks north, a pedestrian confined to the street grid walks 3 blocks then 4 blocks — 7 blocks total, the L1 norm's intuition. A bird flies the straight-line distance instead — 5 units, the L2 norm's intuition. Both are completely valid answers to "how far is that point from here"; they just measure different things about the same trip.

Analogy boundaryNorms are pure arithmetic on a list of numbers, regardless of whether any real streets or straight lines are involved. The city-blocks-versus-flight framing only carries the L1-versus-L2 distinction, nothing about actual geography or travel.

06
MECHANISM

Tracing all three computations on t = (3, 4)

Dimension — count the components: 2.
L1 norm — |3| + |4| = 3 + 4 = 7.
L2 norm — square each (9, 16), sum them (25), take the square root: √25 = 5.
Normalize — divide each original component by 5: (3/5, 4/5) = (0.6, 0.8).
Verify — the normalized vector's own L2 norm: √(0.6² + 0.8²) = √(0.36 + 0.64) = √1 = 1. Confirmed.

09
MATHEMATICS

Three formulas and one boundary case

For a vector v ∈ ℝⁿ with components v₁ through vₙ:

dim(v) = n ‖v‖₁ = |v₁| + |v₂| + … + |vₙ| = Σᵢ |vᵢ| ‖v‖₂ = √(v₁² + v₂² + … + vₙ²) = √(Σᵢ vᵢ²) v̂ = v / ‖v‖₂, so that ‖v̂‖₂ = 1

Read the first line as a count, the second and third as sums built from the vector's own components, and the fourth as a rescaling that divides every component by the same positive number. One boundary case matters: if v is the zero vector, ‖v‖₂ = 0, and dividing by zero is undefined — the zero vector has no direction to preserve, so it cannot be normalized at all.

11
CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, a counterexample, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

Section 03's ticket vector t = (3, 4)

A textbook case — non-zero, low-dimensional, easy to verify by hand with the classic 3-4-5 triangle.

BOUNDARY CASE

The zero vector, (0, 0)

Dimension 2, L1 norm 0, L2 norm 0 — and no normalized version exists, because Section 09's division by ‖v‖₂ is undefined at zero.

COUNTEREXAMPLE

Assuming L1-normalizing gives a unit vector

Dividing (3, 4) by its L1 norm (7) gives (3/7, 4/7) ≈ (0.429, 0.571). Its L2 norm is √(0.184+0.327) ≈ 0.714, not 1 — L1-normalizing does not produce an L2 unit vector.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

"A bigger L1 norm means a bigger L2 norm"

(5, 0) has L1 = 5, L2 = 5. (3, 3) has L1 = 6 (bigger) but L2 ≈ 4.24 (smaller) — a bigger L1 norm does not guarantee a bigger L2 norm across different vectors.

12
CODE

Dimension, both norms, and a guarded normalize, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES, WITH A ZERO-VECTOR GUARD 
def dimension(v):
    return len(v)

def l1_norm(v):
    # Section 05's Sum |v_i|
    return sum(abs(x) for x in v)

def l2_norm(v):
    # Section 05's sqrt(Sum v_i^2)
    return sum(x * x for x in v) ** 0.5

def normalize(v):
    # Section 09's boundary case: the zero vector has no direction to
    # rescale, so dividing by its own (zero) L2 norm is refused outright
    # instead of silently producing NaN or infinity.
    norm = l2_norm(v)
    if norm == 0:
        raise ValueError("cannot normalize the zero vector")
    return [x / norm for x in v]


t = [3, 4]
print(dimension(t))     # 2
print(l1_norm(t))        # 7
print(l2_norm(t))        # 5.0
print(normalize(t))      # [0.6, 0.8]

assert dimension(t) == 2
assert l1_norm(t) == 7
assert l2_norm(t) == 5.0
assert normalize(t) == [0.6, 0.8]
assert abs(l2_norm(normalize(t)) - 1.0) < 1e-9

try:
    normalize([0, 0])
    assert False, "should have raised"
except ValueError:
    pass  # the zero vector correctly refuses to normalize
13
ECOSYSTEM

Where production code exposes the same three ideas

NumPy's numpy.linalg.norm(v) computes the L2 norm by default, and numpy.linalg.norm(v, ord=1) switches to the L1 norm — Section 05's two formulas, as one function with a parameter instead of two separate ones. Dividing by that result, v / numpy.linalg.norm(v), is Section 09's normalization written as one line. Embedding libraries built on top of models like sentence-transformers commonly expose a normalize_embeddings option that performs exactly this L2 normalization automatically — named here only as a forward pointer to Module 08, not explained.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes — of computing and using norms

DIVIDE BY ZERO

Normalizing the zero vector

Section 09's boundary case, unhandled: dividing by a zero L2 norm crashes or silently produces NaN/infinity instead of raising a clear error like Section 12's guard.

WRONG NORM, SAME NAME

Calling an L1-normalized vector "normalized" without saying which norm

Section 11's counterexample: it looks rescaled, but its L2 norm isn't 1 — any code downstream assuming a unit L2 vector gets a silently wrong number.

SCALE MISMATCH

Comparing norms across different-dimension vectors as if they meant the same thing

A higher-dimensional vector's L1 norm tends to run larger purely from having more terms to sum — comparing raw norms across dimensions without accounting for that is comparing different scales.

FLOATING-POINT DRIFT

A "unit" vector whose computed norm is 0.999999999, not exactly 1

Ordinary floating-point rounding after repeated division — expected, and usually handled with a small tolerance rather than an exact equality check, as Section 12's assertion does.

CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
Normalizing always means dividing by the L1 normThis concept's default is the L2 norm; L1-normalization is a separate, named idea used for a different purpose (Section 05), not the default meaning of "normalize."
A vector's dimension can change after it's normalizedDimension only counts components; normalizing rescales their values, so the count — and therefore the dimension — never changes.

18
REALITY

Production implications, at introductory depth

  • Storing a vector's precomputed norm alongside it avoids recomputing Section 09's square root on every later use — a small cost saving that adds up across a large index (Module 09, forward flavor only).
  • An index that mixes normalized and unnormalized vectors breaks any later comparison that assumes a consistent convention — Section 16's scale-mismatch risk applies at the whole-index level, not just one vector at a time.
  • Section 16's floating-point drift is routine at scale; production normalization code checks against a small tolerance rather than expecting an exact 1.0.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

State the formulas for the L1 norm and the L2 norm of a vector v with components v₁ through vₙ.
‖v‖₁ = |v₁| + |v₂| + … + |vₙ|. ‖v‖₂ = √(v₁² + v₂² + … + vₙ²).
Why can't the zero vector be normalized?
Normalization divides every component by the vector's L2 norm. The zero vector's L2 norm is 0, and dividing by zero is undefined — there's also no meaningful direction for the zero vector to preserve.
Why does dividing a vector by its L1 norm not generally produce a vector with L2 norm 1?
The L1 and L2 norms are different formulas that usually give different values for the same vector, so dividing by one doesn't rescale the vector to length 1 as measured by the other — Section 11's (3, 4) example confirms this by hand.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

A teammate says two embedding vectors "must be similar" because they have the same L2 norm. What's wrong with that reasoning?
Equal L2 norm only means equal magnitude — countless different directions share the same length. Nothing about magnitude alone says whether two vectors point the same way or are otherwise similar; that requires comparing direction, which is Concept 03's dot product and cosine similarity, not a norm.
Why does normalization code typically guard against the zero vector explicitly, rather than letting the division happen?
Dividing by an L2 norm of zero is undefined and would either crash or silently produce NaN or infinity; an explicit guard (Section 12) turns a hidden failure into a clear, catchable error, and makes the zero vector's undefined direction visible in the code rather than hidden in its output.
Design question: your team is building a vector index where some embeddings were normalized before storage and some weren't, due to a bug. What immediate risk does that create, and how would you detect it cheaply?
Any later step assuming a consistent norm convention (comparisons, thresholds, or distance calculations built on top of a unit-norm assumption) silently gets inconsistent results depending on which vectors happen to be involved. A cheap detection pass: compute the L2 norm of every stored vector and check whether they cluster near 1.0 (normalized) or vary widely (not normalized) — vectors far from the expected cluster flag the ones the bug touched.
