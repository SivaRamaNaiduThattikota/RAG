01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Exact / brute-force / exhaustive / flat / linear-scan -- five names, one mechanism

EXACT (this concept)

Score the query against every stored vector, one by one, then sort. Guaranteed correct -- nothing is skipped.

APPROXIMATE (Concept 02, named only)

Skip most of the corpus on purpose, trading a small amount of guaranteed exactness for sub-linear query time. Not covered here.

First memory: exact search means comparing against everyone -- the cost is real and grows with the corpus, but the result is always correct.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

The step Module 04's own callout declined to take

Module 04 Concept 09 already derived O(d) per score, O(n·d) for a full brute-force search, and O(n log n) to sort the results -- on paper, for 3-4 hypothetical candidates. That concept's own Section 04 callout said plainly that a real system's actual milliseconds also depend on hardware, something Big-O deliberately abstracts away, and stopped there. Module 08 then built real embedding vectors for millions of real chunks. This concept is the first point where "compute similarity against every stored vector" stops being a hypothetical exercise on paper and the course finally asks: at real corpus scale, how expensive is that, in actual seconds?

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

1,000,000 vectors, 1,024 dimensions, one real number

A corpus holds n = 1,000,000 stored vectors at d = 1,024 dimensions each -- a common real embedding size (Cohere embed-english-v3, BGE-large and Voyage-2 all embed near this scale). One score costs roughly d = 1,024 operations (the simple beginner count). A full brute-force search against every stored vector costs n × d = 1,000,000 × 1,024 = 1,024,000,000 operations (1.024 billion). At a cited real-hardware rate of 484 GFLOPS (a stock AMD Ryzen 5 3600 CPU, from Wikipedia's FLOPS page), that search takes 1,024,000,000 ÷ 484,000,000,000 ≈ 2.12 milliseconds. Sorting the 1,000,000 resulting scores costs about 1,000,000 × log₂(1,000,000) ≈ 19.93 million comparisons -- a cost that depends on n, not on d. Storing all 1,000,000 vectors costs 1,000,000 × 1,024 × 4 bytes = 4.096 GB.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Score everyone, then sort

For each of the n stored vectors, compute its cosine or dot-product score against the query -- Concept 03's mechanism, unchanged. Once every one of the n vectors has a score, sort them (Concept 08's ranking, cited) and keep the top-k. Nothing is skipped -- that is the exactness guarantee, stated plainly: exact search is guaranteed to find the true top-k, at that cost.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Big-O numbers are already milliseconds."

False -- O(n·d) is an operation count, not a time. This concept is where the real millisecond number finally appears, by dividing that count by a real hardware rate.

"Exact/brute-force search is always too slow for production."

False -- the worked example above shows single-digit milliseconds at a genuinely real, deployable scale (1,000,000 vectors).

"A vector database's job is always approximate search."

False -- FAISS's own IndexFlatL2 and IndexFlatIP exist specifically for exact search, by deliberate choice, not as a fallback.

WORKED EXAMPLE

1,000,000 vectors to 1,000,000,000 -- and the budget crossover

Two conventions, side by side -- mirroring Concept 09's own precedent (its beginner section used the simpler d-ops proxy; its advanced Section 12 code used the exact 2d−1 count; the two differ by a constant factor of roughly 2×, which does not change the O(n·d) classification).

Beginner / simple-proxy convention (one score ≈ d ops): one score = 1,024 ops. Full search = 1,000,000 × 1,024 = 1,024,000,000 ops. Wall-clock at 484 GFLOPS: 1,024,000,000 ÷ 484,000,000,000 ≈ 2.12 ms.

Advanced / exact convention (one score = 2d−1 ops, matching Concept 09's dot_product_ops(d) = d + (d−1) exactly): one score = 2×1,024−1 = 2,047 ops. Full search = 1,000,000 × 2,047 = 2,047,000,000 ops. Wall-clock: 2,047,000,000 ÷ 484,000,000,000 ≈ 4.23 ms. Ratio exact/simple = 1.999... ≈ 2×, exactly reproducing Concept 09's own stated constant-factor gap.

Sort (Concept 09's O(n log n), independent of d, cited): 1,000,000 × log₂(1,000,000) ≈ 19,931,569 comparisons. Storage (Concept 09's O(n·d) space, cited): 1,000,000 × 1,024 × 4 = 4.096 GB.

Crossover. At n=1M and n=10M, one query's brute-force search (4.23 ms / 42.29 ms) comfortably fits inside every common interactive-latency budget checked (50 ms/100 ms/300 ms). At n=100M, 422.93 ms exceeds all three. At n=1B, 4.23 s exceeds all three by more than an order of magnitude. Against a 100 ms budget, the crossover lands between n=10M and n=100M -- the concrete point where exact search stops being "obviously fine."

Inverse calculation (feeds the lab): n_max(budget, d) = floor(budget_seconds × RATE ÷ (2d−1)). At d=1,024 and a 100 ms budget: n_max = floor(0.1 × 484,000,000,000 ÷ 2,047) = 23,644,357 vectors -- brute force can handle up to ~23.6 million 1,024-dimension vectors within a 100 ms per-query budget on this cited hardware rate, before exceeding it.

Verification methodEvery figure above was computed and cross-checked via three independent Node.js scripts: (1) a direct formula pass over n=1M/10M/100M/1B at d=1,024; (2) a structurally different manual multiply-loop-plus-add-loop simulation of one score's exact operation count, asserted equal to the closed-form 2d−1 (2,047 = 2,047); (3) a forward/inverse consistency check -- computing n_max for several budgets and dimensions, then plugging it back into the forward time formula and confirming the result lands at or just under the budget (0.09999999747727273 s against a 0.1 s budget).

CODE

A linear scan and a wall-clock estimator, from scratch

PYTHON · FROM SCRATCH -- REUSES CONCEPT 09'S EXACT 2d−1 CONVENTION 
def cosine(u, v):
    dot = sum(a * b for a, b in zip(u, v))
    norm_u = sum(a * a for a in u) ** 0.5
    norm_v = sum(b * b for b in v) ** 0.5
    return dot / (norm_u * norm_v)

def linear_scan(query, vectors, k):
    # Score EVERY stored vector -- nothing is skipped.
    scored = [(vec_id, cosine(query, vec)) for vec_id, vec in vectors]
    scored.sort(key=lambda pair: pair[1], reverse=True)
    return scored[:k]

def estimate_wall_clock(n, d, flops_rate):
    ops_per_score = 2 * d - 1          # d multiplications + (d-1) additions
    total_ops = n * ops_per_score
    return total_ops / flops_rate       # seconds

RATE = 484e9  # AMD Ryzen 5 3600, 484 GFLOPS, Wikipedia's FLOPS page

assert round(estimate_wall_clock(1_000_000, 1024, RATE) * 1000, 4) == 4.2293
assert round(estimate_wall_clock(1_000_000_000, 1024, RATE), 3) == 4.229

# Edge case: asking for more results than exist should not crash.
tiny_corpus = [(1, [0.1, 0.2]), (2, [0.3, 0.4])]
assert len(linear_scan([0.2, 0.3], tiny_corpus, k=10)) == 2

19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why doesn't sort's cost depend on d?
Sorting compares already-computed scores against each other -- each comparison is O(1) regardless of how many dimensions produced the score. Only computing the scores themselves (O(n·d)) depends on d.
Why is exact search still valuable even after Concept 02 introduces approximate methods?
Exact search is the only guaranteed-correct option, and it remains genuinely fast enough for real corpora up to tens of millions of vectors (Section 10's own crossover point) -- it isn't obsolete, it's a baseline with a known, computable range of usefulness.
How do you compute a wall-clock estimate from n, d and a FLOPS rate?
operations = n × (2d−1); time_seconds = operations ÷ RATE. Multiply by 1,000 for milliseconds.

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why is exact search "exact"?
Because it compares the query against every single stored vector before ranking -- nothing is skipped, so the returned top-k is guaranteed to be the true top-k, not an estimate.
Mathematical: verify a wall-clock figure from the scaling table.
At n=100,000,000 and d=1,024: ops = 100,000,000 × 2,047 = 204,700,000,000. Time = 204,700,000,000 ÷ 484,000,000,000 ≈ 422.93 ms, matching the table.
Coding: implement linear_scan.
Section 12 gives the full from-scratch implementation -- score every stored vector with cosine similarity, sort descending, return the top-k slice.
Debugging: a service's brute-force search suddenly got slower. What's the first thing to check?
Whether n (corpus size) or d (embedding dimension) changed -- both feed directly into the O(n·d) cost, and either one growing explains a slowdown without anything being "broken."
System design: at what corpus size would you start evaluating an approximate index, and what would you measure first?
As soon as a real wall-clock estimate (Section 09's formula) approaches your interactive latency budget -- for this concept's own cited hardware and d=1,024, that's roughly the 10-100 million vector range. Measure actual query latency under real load first, not just the theoretical estimate, before committing to an approximate index's added complexity.
