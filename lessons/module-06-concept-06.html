MODULE 06 · CONCEPT 06

The same fact, said twice.
Which copy survives?

Concept 05 cleaned noise that existed WITHIN one record. This concept covers the same content entering a corpus more than once ACROSS records — the same PDF attached to two different tickets, or a policy mirrored on a wiki with a word or two changed. Three distinct techniques apply: exact deduplication (hash equality on already-cleaned content), near-deduplication (fuzzy similarity for copies that are close but not byte-identical), and canonicalization — picking one authoritative survivor once duplicates are found, reusing Concept 01's authority ranking exactly as it stands, not inventing a new rule.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Three separate techniques, one shared goal

THIS CONCEPT

Exact dedup (hash equality across records), near-dedup (fuzzy similarity for reworded copies), and canonicalization (picking one authoritative survivor among duplicates or near-duplicates).

NOT THIS CONCEPT

Concept 05's within-record noise (encoding, boilerplate, whitespace) and Concept 07's same-document-over-time versioning — both different problems, named only as neighbors here.

First memory: exact dedup asks "are these byte-identical?" Near-dedup asks "are these close enough to count as the same thing?" Canonicalization asks "given a cluster of duplicates, which one do we actually keep?" Three different questions, in that order.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

The same fact, indexed twice, wastes retrieval's limited budget

A support ticket's PDF attachment can get re-attached to a second, related ticket. A policy bulletin can get mirrored onto an internal wiki, with a word or two rephrased along the way. If both copies enter the corpus as separate records, retrieval can waste its limited top-k budget returning the same fact twice instead of two genuinely different pieces of evidence — and near-duplicates that drift slightly can even look like two sources quietly disagreeing, when they're really one fact said twice. This concept exists to catch both the exact and the near case, then resolve the resulting cluster down to one record worth trusting.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

One retention bulletin, three copies, one survivor

R1: a PDF attached to Ticket #4821 ("...retained for 24 months after case closure"), an official policy document, 2 months old. R2: the exact same PDF re-attached to Ticket #5190, 3 weeks old. R3: the same policy mirrored on the internal wiki with one word changed ("...retained for 24 months once case closure"), 1 week old — the newest of the three.

EXACT DEDUP

R1 and R2 hash identically

Byte-for-byte identical content — a cryptographic hash of both produces the exact same fingerprint.

NEAR DEDUP

R1 and R3 are 50% similar, not identical

One word changed out of eleven — close, but a hash treats them as completely unrelated.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Hashing alone catches all duplicates."

A single changed word produces a completely unrelated hash (the avalanche effect) — hashing has zero tolerance for near-duplicates by design.

"Any similarity threshold works for near-dedup."

Too loose a threshold merges genuinely distinct records; too strict a threshold lets true near-duplicates slip through as independent — Section 14's lab makes this trade-off directly visible.

"Canonicalization means deleting the losing copies."

Usually the non-canonical records are tombstoned or pointed at the canonical one, not hard-deleted — the audit trail matters for later governance.

"The newest copy is always the canonical one."

Section 03's own example: R3 is the newest of the three, but Concept 01's authority ranking checks type first — a wiki mirror still ranks below an official document regardless of recency.

04
INTUITION

A fingerprint, a family resemblance, and a file clerk

Exact dedup is a fingerprint match — either two prints are identical, or they're not, with nothing in between. Near-dedup is a family resemblance — two photos of related people that share enough features to flag for a closer look, without being the same photo. Canonicalization is a file clerk who, once told "these three folders are about the same case," picks exactly one to keep on top of the stack, using a fixed, published rule rather than personal judgment.

Analogy boundaryThe family-resemblance analogy explains why near-dedup needs a similarity score instead of a yes/no check. It doesn't explain how that score is actually computed -- Section 09 gives the exact Jaccard formula, not just the shape of the idea.

06
MECHANISM

Three lanes, one shared handoff

Exact-dedup lane — normalize content, compute a cryptographic hash (e.g. SHA-256), and group records with identical hashes.
Near-dedup lane — build k-word shingle sets for records not already caught by exact matching, compute pairwise Jaccard similarity, and flag pairs above a chosen threshold.
Canonicalization lane — for every resolved cluster (exact or near), apply Concept 01's auth(s) = (type_rank, −recency) and keep the record with the best score.
Handoff — one canonical record per cluster moves forward; non-canonical records are tombstoned or pointed at the canonical one, not hard-deleted, preserving the audit trail.

09
MATHEMATICS

Why one changed word produces a completely different hash

A cryptographic hash function is designed for the avalanche effect: flipping even a single input bit changes roughly half the output bits, unpredictably. Formally, there is no relationship between h(x) and h(x′) for two inputs x, x′ that differ by even one character — h(x) ≠ h(x′) essentially always, with no partial credit for "almost the same." This is exactly why exact dedup needs a completely separate mechanism (shingling + Jaccard) for near-duplicates: hashing is designed to make similarity invisible, not to measure it. Jaccard similarity, by contrast, is explicitly a graded measure: J(A,B) = |A∩B| / |A∪B| ranges continuously from 0 (no shared shingles) to 1 (identical shingle sets), which is precisely why it can express "50% similar" in a way a hash never can.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Three records, computed by script, not by hand

R1 = "complaint records must be retained for 24 months after case closure" (official PDF, age 2 months). R2 = byte-identical copy of R1 (age 3 weeks). R3 = "complaint records must be retained for 24 months once case closure" (wiki mirror, age 1 week) — one word changed from R1.

PairCheckResult
R1 vs. R2SHA-256 hash equality3ad197ae…15105e02 = 3ad197ae…15105e02 → MATCH
R1 vs. R3SHA-256 hash equality3ad197ae…15105e02 ≠ 1c109b99…a933de8 → NO MATCH
R1 vs. R3Word-trigram Jaccard6 shared trigrams / 12 total = 0.50

Both R1 and R3 tokenize to 11 words, producing 9 overlapping word-trigrams each; 6 trigrams are shared (everything not touching the single changed word), and the union across both sets is 12, giving J = 6/12 = 0.50 exactly. Canonicalization over the full cluster {R1, R2, R3} using Concept 01's auth(s) = (type_rank, −recency): R1 and R2 share type_rank 1 (official document), beating R3's type_rank 2 (wiki mirror) regardless of R3 being the newest record. Within rank 1, R2 (3 weeks) beats R1 (2 months) on recency. Canonical record: R2. Every value here was computed via an executed Node.js script (crypto.createHash for the hashes, a hand-rolled shingle/Jaccard function) and independently cross-checked in Python.

11
CONCRETE USE

Two clean fits, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

A byte-identical PDF re-attached to a second ticket

Caught immediately by exact hashing — no fuzzy matching needed at all.

CLEAN FIT

A reworded wiki mirror of the same policy

Caught by shingle/Jaccard similarity exactly as Section 10 demonstrated on R1 vs. R3.

BOUNDARY CASE

Two genuinely different records that happen to share a lot of boilerplate

Shared templated language can inflate Jaccard similarity between records that aren't actually duplicates — a real false-positive risk worth checking, not assuming away.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Skipping exact hashing and relying only on fuzzy matching

Fuzzy matching is more expensive to compute and unnecessary for the (common) byte-identical case — always run the cheap exact check first.

12
CODE

Exact dedup, near dedup and canonicalization, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES BEYOND HASHLIB 
import hashlib

def content_hash(text):
    return hashlib.sha256(text.encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()

def shingles(text, k=3):
    words = text.split(" ")
    return {" ".join(words[i:i+k]) for i in range(len(words) - k + 1)}

def jaccard(a, b):
    return len(a & b) / len(a | b)

def authority_key(record):
    return (record["type_rank"], -record["age_days"])

def canonicalize(cluster):
    return min(cluster, key=authority_key)

R1 = "complaint records must be retained for 24 months after case closure"
R2 = R1
R3 = "complaint records must be retained for 24 months once case closure"

assert content_hash(R1) == content_hash(R2)
assert content_hash(R1) != content_hash(R3)

j = jaccard(shingles(R1), shingles(R3))
assert round(j, 2) == 0.50

cluster = [
    {"id": "R1", "type_rank": 1, "age_days": 60},
    {"id": "R2", "type_rank": 1, "age_days": 21},
    {"id": "R3", "type_rank": 2, "age_days": 7},
]
assert canonicalize(cluster)["id"] == "R2"
13
ECOSYSTEM

The same three lanes, in real tools

Exact dedup: any language's standard cryptographic hash library (Python's hashlib, Node's crypto) computes SHA-256 directly. Near dedup at scale: computing pairwise Jaccard over every record pair is O(n²) and infeasible for large corpora — datasketch's MinHash and LSH (locality-sensitive hashing) approximate Jaccard similarity while only comparing likely-similar candidates, the standard production technique named here but not derived. Canonicalization and broader entity resolution: record-linkage libraries formalize the "which record survives" decision at scale, generalizing Concept 01's simple two-factor ranking to richer scoring rules when needed.

DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

THRESHOLD TOO LOOSE

Distinct records get merged as duplicates

A low similarity threshold can flag genuinely different records as near-duplicates purely from shared boilerplate.

THRESHOLD TOO STRICT

True near-duplicates slip through as independent

A high threshold misses real near-duplicates, letting the same fact re-enter the corpus as if it were new evidence.

STALE AUTHORITY INPUT

A wrong canonical pick from outdated type/recency data

If a record's type_rank or age is wrong or stale, canonicalization confidently picks the wrong survivor — the formula is only as good as its inputs.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Exact hashing is cheap enough to run on every ingested record with no real cost concern; near-dedup at real corpus scale needs LSH-style banding to avoid comparing every pair directly.
  • A similarity threshold is a genuine tuning decision, not a fixed constant — it should be validated against real near-duplicate and non-duplicate pairs from the actual corpus, not assumed.
  • Maintaining canonical-record pointers has to survive re-ingestion — if a source record changes, its cluster membership and canonical pick may need to be recomputed, not assumed permanent.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why can't hashing detect that R1 and R3 are 50% similar?
Cryptographic hashes are designed for the avalanche effect -- any change to the input, however small, produces a completely unrelated output with no partial similarity preserved. Hashing can only answer "identical or not," never "how similar."
Why does R2, not R3, end up as the canonical record, even though R3 is the newest?
Concept 01's authority ranking checks type first: R1 and R2 are official documents (type_rank 1), which beats R3's wiki-mirror status (type_rank 2) regardless of recency. Only within the same type rank does recency act as the tiebreaker.
Why must Concept 05's cleaning run before this concept's dedup check?
Comparing records that still contain within-record noise (inconsistent whitespace, encoding artifacts) would make two truly identical records hash differently, or make two genuinely different records look artificially more similar -- dedup needs already-normalized input to produce meaningful results.

21
FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
N1 and N2 differ by one word ("two" vs. "three"), so they do NOT hash-match. Both tokenize to 13 words, producing 11 trigrams each. The changed word affects 3 trigrams (the windows containing position 10 of 13), so 8 trigrams are shared; union = 11+11−8 = 14. Jaccard = 8/14 ≈ 0.571. At a 0.60 threshold, this pair would NOT be flagged as a near-duplicate (0.571 < 0.60) -- just under the line, illustrating exactly how sensitive the flag/no-flag verdict is to where the threshold sits.

Teach-back: explain, in your own words, why a single-word change near the middle of a sentence affects more trigrams than the same change at the very start or end.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Why can't a hash-based deduplication pipeline catch a reworded near-duplicate?
A cryptographic hash is designed so any input change, however small, produces a completely unrelated output (the avalanche effect) -- it can only answer "identical or not," with no notion of partial similarity. Catching reworded copies needs a separate, graded similarity measure like shingle-based Jaccard similarity.
How would you scale near-duplicate detection to millions of records without comparing every pair?
Use locality-sensitive hashing techniques like MinHash/LSH banding, which approximate Jaccard similarity while only comparing candidate pairs likely to be similar, avoiding the O(n²) cost of direct pairwise comparison.
A teammate wants to permanently delete every non-canonical duplicate record immediately. What would you push back on?
That deleting removes the audit trail and makes the decision irreversible -- tombstoning or pointing non-canonical records at the canonical one preserves the history while still keeping retrieval clean, and is recoverable if a canonicalization decision later turns out to be wrong.
How would you choose a near-duplicate similarity threshold for a real corpus?
Validate it against a labeled sample of known near-duplicate and known-distinct pairs from the actual corpus, checking both false-merge and false-miss rates, rather than assuming a threshold that worked elsewhere transfers directly -- Section 16's threshold-too-loose/too-strict failure modes are exactly what that validation is meant to catch.
