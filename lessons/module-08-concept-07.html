MODULE 08 · CONCEPT 07

Unrelated words.
A suspiciously narrow cone.

Concepts 01-06 all silently assumed cosine similarity behaves the same way everywhere in the embedding space -- every cosine number computed so far was taken at face value. This concept names the real pathology that breaks that assumption: anisotropy. Real trained embedding spaces are often NOT spread uniformly over the unit hypersphere -- they cluster in a narrow cone, so even semantically unrelated texts can show an artificially high baseline cosine. Four illustrative, completely unrelated words -- pizza, orbit, cello, tax -- placed at close angles show a mean pairwise cosine of 0.990608, despite sharing no meaning at all. Spread the same idea evenly instead, and the mean drops to -0.333333. Concept 06's normalization cannot fix this: cosine similarity was already blind to each vector's own length before normalizing ever ran, proved here both algebraically and to 10 decimal places.

02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Every prior cosine number was taken at face value

Concepts 01-06 computed real cosine values (0.9755, a 0.6644 tie, a full pooling cross-table) and treated each one as direct evidence of similarity. This concept doesn't contradict any of those specific claims -- it asks a new question none of them raised: is the whole space those numbers live in actually well-behaved?

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Four unrelated words, one suspiciously narrow cone

Four illustrative, completely unrelated words -- pizza, orbit, cello, tax -- placed at close angles (20°-33°) with arbitrary, irrelevant magnitudes. All six pairwise cosines land above 0.97, mean 0.990608 -- despite the four words sharing no meaning whatsoever. Spread the identical idea evenly instead (north/east/south/west), and the mean drops to -0.333333.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"If my vectors are all normalized, cosine similarity is safely comparable everywhere in the space."

False -- Section 09/10's proof: normalizing cannot change anisotropy at all.

"An anisotropic space means the embedding model is broken."

False -- it's common and measurable; real fixes (BERT-flow, BERT-whitening) exist precisely because it's fixable.

"A fixed cosine threshold like 0.75 works the same across any embedding model."

False -- anisotropy undermines the portability of any fixed absolute threshold across models or domains.

09
MATHEMATICS

Why normalizing cannot change cosine similarity at all

cosine(u,v) = dot(u,v) / (‖u‖·‖v‖)
Let û = u/‖u‖: cosine(û,v) = dot(û,v)/(‖û‖·‖v‖) = (dot(u,v)/‖u‖)/(1·‖v‖) = dot(u,v)/(‖u‖·‖v‖) = cosine(u,v)

The two expressions are algebraically IDENTICAL, not merely close -- cosine similarity was already invariant to each vector's own length before normalizing ever ran. For genuinely random unit vectors in d dimensions, E[cosine between two independent random unit vectors] = 0, with the fluctuation shrinking as dimension grows (cited qualitatively as background, not derived here) -- this 2D 4-point illustration is a simplified stand-in for that higher-dimensional fact.

10
WORKED EXAMPLE

Three parts: the cone, the contrast, and the proof

Part 1 -- the anisotropic narrow cone. Four unrelated illustrative words at close angles, arbitrary irrelevant magnitudes: cello=20°,1.1; pizza=25°,1.4; tax=29°,1.8; orbit=33°,2.1. All six pairwise cosines: pizza·orbit=0.990268, pizza·cello=0.996195, pizza·tax=0.997564, orbit·cello=0.974370, orbit·tax=0.997564, cello·tax=0.987688. Mean = 0.990608. Framing: these four words share no semantic relationship whatsoever, yet a naive reading of "cosine similarity" would suggest they're almost identical.

Part 2 -- the isotropic contrast. Four unit vectors at 0°/90°/180°/270° (north/east/south/west). Pairwise cosines: north·east=0, north·south=−1, north·west=0, east·south=0, east·west=−1, south·west=0. Mean = −0.333333. Under genuine isotropy, unrelated directions are just as likely to be perpendicular or opposite as aligned, so the baseline sits near zero or negative -- nothing like Part 1's 0.990608.

Part 3 -- normalization cannot fix anisotropy. Numeric check on pizza vs. orbit: cosine BEFORE normalizing = 0.9902680687. pizza_hat = pizza/‖pizza‖, orbit_hat = orbit/‖orbit‖ (both ‖·‖=1.0000000000). dot(pizza_hat, orbit_hat) AFTER normalizing = 0.9902680687 -- identical to 10 decimal places, exactly as the algebra predicts.

Verification method: every Part 1/2 cosine was computed twice by two algebraically independent routes -- the standard dot-product/norm formula applied to (x,y) coordinates, and the pure trigonometric identity cosine = cos(angle_a − angle_b), which uses no dot product or norm at all -- matching to 6 decimal places on every value. The Part 3 identity was verified to 10 decimal places with no floating-point drift.

12
CODE

The cone, the contrast, and the normalization proof, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
import math

def to_vec(angle_deg, mag):
    r = math.radians(angle_deg)
    return (mag * math.cos(r), mag * math.sin(r))

def dot(u, v):
    return u[0]*v[0] + u[1]*v[1]

def norm(u):
    return math.sqrt(dot(u, u))

def cosine(u, v):
    return dot(u, v) / (norm(u) * norm(v))

def normalize(u):
    n = norm(u)
    return (u[0]/n, u[1]/n)

cello, pizza, tax, orbit = to_vec(20,1.1), to_vec(25,1.4), to_vec(29,1.8), to_vec(33,2.1)
pairs = [(pizza,orbit),(pizza,cello),(pizza,tax),(orbit,cello),(orbit,tax),(cello,tax)]
mean_cos = sum(cosine(a,b) for a,b in pairs) / len(pairs)
assert round(mean_cos, 6) == 0.990608

pizza_hat, orbit_hat = normalize(pizza), normalize(orbit)
assert round(cosine(pizza, orbit), 10) == round(dot(pizza_hat, orbit_hat), 10)

ECOSYSTEM

The real papers naming and fixing this

Ethayarajh (2019), "How Contextual are Contextualized Word Representations?" defines and measures anisotropy in BERT/ELMo/GPT-2 representations via self-similarity -- the primary citation for the phenomenon's definition and measurement. Li, Zhou, He, Wang, Yang & Li (2020), the BERT-flow paper, shows BERT induces a non-smooth anisotropic sentence space and proposes normalizing flows to map it toward an isotropic Gaussian. Su, Cao, Liu & Ou (2021), the BERT-whitening paper, names anisotropy as "a critical bottleneck" and shows a classical whitening transform improves isotropy competitively with BERT-flow while also reducing dimensionality -- connecting back to Concept 06's own storage-cost table.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Anisotropy undermines the portability of any fixed absolute cosine threshold across models or domains -- a "0.75 = relevant" rule tuned on one encoder won't transfer.
  • It does not necessarily break relative ranking as long as true matches stay measurably closer than distractors, but a narrow high baseline shrinks the discriminative margin, making top-k cutoffs more fragile near ties.
  • Whitening/flow post-processing adds a small, one-time preprocessing cost, not a per-query cost.

RECALL

Knowledge check

Define anisotropy and isotropy.
Isotropy: a vector population's directions spread uniformly over the unit hypersphere. Anisotropy: they don't -- they cluster.
Why can't normalizing fix anisotropy?
Cosine similarity is already invariant to each vector's own length -- cosine(û,v)=cosine(u,v) algebraically, for any u. Normalizing only rescales length; anisotropy is about direction.
Name one real remediation technique.
BERT-flow (a learned normalizing flow) or BERT-whitening (a closed-form linear transform).

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why does anisotropy matter for retrieval?
It inflates the baseline cosine among unrelated items, making any fixed absolute threshold unreliable and shrinking the discriminative margin between true matches and distractors.
Mathematical: prove normalization can't fix anisotropy.
cosine(u,v)=dot(u,v)/(‖u‖‖v‖). Substituting û=u/‖u‖ gives cosine(û,v)=dot(u,v)/(‖u‖‖v‖)=cosine(u,v) exactly -- the normalized and unnormalized cosines are algebraically identical, so rescaling length changes nothing about direction-based similarity.
Coding: implement the cone example and the normalization-invariance assertion.
Section 12 gives the full from-scratch implementation, with an assert confirming the normalized and unnormalized cosine match exactly.
Debugging: a retriever's cosine scores are all suspiciously high, even for irrelevant results. What do you check?
Measure the mean/baseline cosine across a large random sample of unrelated corpus item pairs -- if it's far from 0, that's the anisotropy signal, not a data or relevance-labeling bug.
System design: how would you choose between BERT-flow and BERT-whitening for a production corpus?
Prefer BERT-whitening when you want a fast, closed-form fix with no training loop and a bonus dimensionality reduction; prefer BERT-flow when a more flexible, learned transform is worth the cost of an unsupervised training pass on your own corpus.
