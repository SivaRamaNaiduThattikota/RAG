01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Tokenizer training and tokenizer use are two different jobs

You already know what a token, a token ID, and a vocabulary are (Module 01, Concept 6). What that lesson left open is where the vocabulary itself comes from, and what actually happens, step by step, when it is built.

Tokenizer training is a one-time, offline process that reads a large text corpus, counts how often symbols sit next to each other, and repeatedly merges the single most frequent pair into a new vocabulary entry until a target vocabulary size is reached. Tokenizer use is the separate, much cheaper step of applying that already-learned list of merges, in order, to a brand-new piece of text at request time. Training happens once per tokenizer; use happens on every request that model ever serves.

First memory: training builds the merge list from frequency counts, once; use only replays that fixed list, every time.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Why the training/use split matters, not just “subwords exist”

Module 01, Concept 6 explained why subword tokenization beats pure character-level or pure word-level splitting. It did not explain how the specific pieces in a real vocabulary get chosen, or why the exact same word can tokenize differently on two different models. Both questions have the same answer: a specific training corpus produced a specific set of merges, and only that exact set of merges is replayed at inference time.

WHAT MODULE 01 ALREADY ESTABLISHED

Subword tokenization is a practical middle point between character-level and word-level splitting; BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece exist as named options.

WHAT THIS CONCEPT ADDS

Exactly how a BPE vocabulary gets built from frequency counts, how WordPiece and SentencePiece choose merges differently, and why digits and rare terms fragment the way they do.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

A tiny vocabulary, built from four words

Picture a trainer given exactly one small corpus: the word low appears 5 times, lower 2 times, newest 6 times, and widest 3 times. Every word starts broken into individual characters plus a marker for the end of the word (written · below). The trainer’s only job is to repeatedly find the single most frequent adjacent pair of symbols anywhere in the corpus and glue it into one new symbol — then do it again.

Word Frequency Starting symbols
low 5 l o w ·
lower 2 l o w e r ·
newest 6 n e w e s t ·
widest 3 w i d e s t ·

Watching just the first move: the pair (e, s) occurs 9 times in total, across newest and widest — more than any other adjacent pair in this corpus — so e and s are glued into one new symbol, es. Section 06 of the advanced layer below carries this exact corpus through five full merges by hand.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

The training loop, generically

Start every word as individual characters plus an end-of-word marker
Count every adjacent symbol pair across the whole corpus, weighted by each word’s frequency
Find the single pair with the highest total count
Replace every occurrence of that pair with one new merged symbol; add it to the vocabulary
Repeat counting and merging until a target vocabulary size or merge count is reached
Save the finished, ordered list of merges — that saved list is the only thing tokenizer USE ever touches again

Nothing here is specific to English, or to any one domain — the algorithm only ever looks at frequencies. That is also its main limitation: it has no idea that est is a linguistically meaningful suffix, only that its two halves kept appearing together in this corpus.

05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

Training runs once; use runs on every request

OFFLINE · TOKENIZER TRAINING
Collect a large representative text corpus
Count adjacent symbol-pair frequencies
Merge the most frequent pair, repeatedly
Stop at a target vocabulary size
Save the ordered merge list
ONLINE · TOKENIZER USE
Receive new text at request time
Split it into characters or bytes
Apply the saved merges, in their trained order
Emit token IDs into the model’s embedding lookup
No counting and no merge decisions happen here

Both lanes sit entirely inside the LLM box and the embedding-model box from Concept 01 — retrieval, Oracle, and orchestration never see a token or a merge decision.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Three training algorithms, three merge rules

Algorithm How it picks the next merge Common confusion
Byte-pair encoding (BPE) Merges the pair with the highest raw weighted frequency count Assumed to use the same rule as WordPiece
WordPiece Merges the pair that most increases likelihood relative to its two parts occurring alone Assumed to be “just Google’s name for BPE”
SentencePiece (unigram mode) Starts from a large candidate set and prunes pieces that hurt a probabilistic model of the corpus Assumed to be one single fixed algorithm rather than a library offering several
Byte-level fallback Falls back to raw UTF-8 bytes for anything the trained vocabulary never learned Assumed to mean every tokenizer splits any given string identically

BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece-unigram are three different training algorithms that all output the same kind of thing — a fixed vocabulary plus a fast way to apply it — but they choose which pieces belong in that vocabulary by three different rules.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often misunderstand

“Tokenizer training happens every time I call the API.”

Training happened once, long before your request; a call only applies the already-saved merges.

“BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece all use the same merge rule.”

They score candidate merges differently — raw frequency, likelihood gain, and corpus probability, respectively.

“A digit is always its own token.”

Digit runs are chunked by whatever grouping was frequent in the training corpus, not by place value.

“A bigger vocabulary is strictly better.”

Every extra vocabulary entry adds a row to the embedding table and the output layer — it is not a free lunch.

“Byte-level fallback means every model tokenizes the same string the same way.”

Fallback only guarantees the string is representable, not that two tokenizers split it identically.

“The training corpus doesn’t matter once you have BPE.”

Merges are learned from that specific corpus’s frequencies — a domain mismatch changes which terms fragment.

08 · YOUR PROJECT MAPPING

Where this lands in the Oracle RAG assistant

VOCABULARY CHOICE Vocab size trades context budget against embedding-table memory

A larger vocabulary shortens sequences but grows the model’s embedding and output layers; both embedding-model and generator choices carry this trade-off.

NUMBER-HEAVY METADATA Never let the LLM compute a number token-by-token

Digit-tokenization quirks mean an LLM can mis-add or mis-compare figures it reads; totals, counts, and dates belong in a real Oracle SQL calculation, not a generated answer.

TRAINING VS USE, IN PRACTICE This project only ever uses pretrained tokenizers

The embedding model and the LLM each ship an already-trained vocabulary; nothing in the RAG pipeline retrains one.

BYTE FALLBACK Guarantees any ingested document text is representable

Unusual encodings or domain symbols in a source document still tokenize into bytes rather than failing outright.

Every concept from here forward keeps assuming token IDs already exist and moves inside the transformer stack that consumes them.

04
INTUITION

An apprentice building a type case by counting, not guessing

Picture an apprentice building a printer’s type case by watching a huge stack of manuscripts: every time two adjacent letters keep showing up glued together, the apprentice casts one combined stamp for that pair instead of two separate ones, then keeps watching for the next most common pair. Given enough manuscripts, common suffixes and whole common words end up as single stamps, purely because they kept recurring — not because the apprentice understands grammar.

Analogy boundaryThe apprentice is greedy and local: each cast is “whatever is most frequent right now,” never a search for the best possible final type case. A different training corpus, or a different tie-breaking rule, produces a different set of stamps — this is exactly why Section 16 treats merge order as a real production risk.
05
PRECISION

Formal definitions

Term Definition Boundary
Tokenizer training An offline process that learns an ordered merge list from corpus frequency counts Runs once per tokenizer version, never at request time
Tokenizer use (encoding) Applying an already-learned merge list, in order, to new input text Runs on every request; makes no new merge decisions
BPE merge criterion Merge the adjacent pair with the highest raw weighted frequency Purely a count; ignores how often the two parts occur alone
WordPiece merge criterion Merge the pair that most increases likelihood relative to its parts occurring independently Requires tracking single-symbol frequencies, not just pair frequencies
score_BPE(a, b) = freq(a, b)
score_WordPiece(a, b) ≈ freq(a, b) / (freq(a) · freq(b))

BPE always merges whichever pair is simply most common. WordPiece’s score is a commonly cited approximation of its actual likelihood criterion: it rewards a pair that co-occurs far more than its two parts’ individual frequencies alone would predict — a pair can lose to a rarer competitor if that competitor’s parts are individually even rarer.

06
MECHANISM

Five merges, traced by hand

Continuing the corpus from Section 03 of the beginner core — low:5, lower:2, newest:6, widest:3 — every step below recounts every adjacent pair from scratch after the previous merge, because a merge can create brand-new pairs that did not exist before it.

Step Top pair (weighted frequency) New symbol
1(e, s) → 6 + 3 = 9es
2(es, t) → 6 + 3 = 9est
3(est, ·) → 6 + 3 = 9est·
4(l, o) → 5 + 2 = 7lo
5(lo, w) → 5 + 2 = 7low

Ties (all three of steps 1–3 sit at weighted frequency 9) are broken here by picking whichever pair’s two symbols come first alphabetically, purely so the trace is reproducible — real implementations often break ties by insertion order instead, which is exactly why two runs over similar corpora can diverge. After step 5, newest and widest both contain the whole symbol est·, and low and lower both contain the whole symbol low — the vocabulary now has 5 new entries beyond the original characters, learned entirely from these four words’ frequencies.

09
MATHEMATICS

Scoring a merge, and measuring what it bought

Symbol Meaning
freq(a, b)Weighted count of adjacent pair (a, b) across the corpus
freq(a), freq(b)Weighted count of symbol a, or b, occurring on its own
|V|Vocabulary size after training
CTotal characters in a piece of text; T its resulting token count
compression(text) = C / T

A larger, better-fitted vocabulary generally raises C / T — more characters packed per token — which is exactly why vocabulary size and typical sequence length trade against each other, a relationship Module 01, Concept 6 already stated qualitatively. What is new here is the mechanism producing |V| itself: it is the direct, countable output of however many merge steps the training loop ran, using whichever of the three scoring rules from Section 05 that algorithm uses.

10
WORKED EXAMPLE

Recomputing pair counts after a merge

Take just the transition from step 3 to step 4 in Section 06. Before step 4, the words stand as: l o w · (×5), l o w e r · (×2), n e w est· (×6), w i d est· (×3).

(l, o): 5 (from low) + 2 (from lower) = 7
(o, w): 5 (from low) + 2 (from lower) = 7
(w, est·): 6 (from newest) + 3 (from widest) = 9 -- but est· was already fully merged in step 3, so this pair does not recur; the next available maximum is the (l, o) / (o, w) tie at 7

Alphabetical tie-break compares the strings "l§o" and "o§w": l precedes o, so (l, o) merges first, exactly as Section 06 reported. This is the same recomputation the interactive lab in Section 14 performs on every click, just on a different corpus.

11
CONCRETE USE

Real vocabulary sizes, and where digits break

SMALL, TARGETED VOCAB

BERT-style WordPiece

Roughly 30,000 pieces — small enough to keep the embedding table modest.

SMALL, LANGUAGE-INDEPENDENT

SentencePiece unigram (Llama-2 class)

Roughly 32,000 pieces, trained directly on raw text without a separate word pre-split.

LARGE, BROAD-COVERAGE

cl100k-style BPE

Roughly 100,000 pieces — more single-token common words and code identifiers, at the cost of a bigger table.

VERY LARGE, MULTILINGUAL-LEANING

o200k-style BPE

Roughly 200,000 pieces — pushes further into shorter sequences for more scripts and symbols.

DIGIT QUIRK

Number grouping is a frequency accident

“48213” and “482134” routinely chunk into different digit groupings, not one-digit-per-token or fixed three-digit groups.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Treating token count as a proxy for numeric precision

More tokens spent on a number says nothing about whether the model will add or compare it correctly.

12
CODE

A real byte-pair-encoding trainer, not a stand-in

PYTHON · FROM-SCRATCH BPE TRAINER 
from collections import Counter

def word_to_symbols(word):
    return list(word) + ["·"]

def pair_counts(vocab):
    # vocab: {tuple_of_symbols: weighted_frequency}
    counts = Counter()
    for symbols, freq in vocab.items():
        for a, b in zip(symbols, symbols[1:]):
            counts[(a, b)] += freq
    return counts

def merge_pair(vocab, pair):
    a, b = pair
    merged = a + b
    new_vocab = {}
    for symbols, freq in vocab.items():
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(symbols):
            if i < len(symbols) - 1 and symbols[i] == a and symbols[i + 1] == b:
                out.append(merged); i += 2
            else:
                out.append(symbols[i]); i += 1
        new_vocab[tuple(out)] = new_vocab.get(tuple(out), 0) + freq
    return new_vocab, merged

corpus = {"low": 5, "lower": 2, "newest": 6, "widest": 3}
vocab = {tuple(word_to_symbols(w)): f for w, f in corpus.items()}
merges = []

for _ in range(5):
    counts = pair_counts(vocab)
    if not counts:
        break
    best_pair = max(counts, key=lambda p: (counts[p], "".join(p)))
    vocab, merged = merge_pair(vocab, best_pair)
    merges.append((best_pair, merged, counts[best_pair]))

print(merges)
# -> [(('e','s'),'es',9), (('es','t'),'est',9), (('est','·'),'est·',9),
#     (('l','o'),'lo',7), (('lo','w'),'low',7)]

This is tokenizer training, running the same loop Section 06 traced by hand — max(..., key=...) is doing the tie-break, choosing the highest count and, on a tie, the alphabetically smallest joined pair. Contrast this with Concept 01’s Section 12, which trained a language model’s next-word counts; here the counting target is adjacent symbol pairs, not next words, and the output is a vocabulary, not a probability table.

ECOSYSTEM

15
CONTRAST

Choosing a training algorithm and a vocabulary size

Approach Wins when Operational cost
Raw BPE, moderate vocab (~30–50k) Fast, simple, well-understood training; general text and code Digit and rare-term fragmentation can still be inconsistent
WordPiece Likelihood-aware merges give slightly better coverage of common morphology Training is more expensive to compute than raw frequency counting
SentencePiece unigram Language-independent input (no whitespace pre-split needed); multilingual corpora Training and probabilistic re-segmentation are the most compute-intensive of the three
Very large BPE vocab (~100–200k) Broad script and symbol coverage; shorter sequences per request Largest embedding table and output-layer memory footprint

None of these rows change whether a generated number should be trusted — that risk, from Section 16, is addressed by pushing calculation into Oracle SQL, not by picking a different tokenizer.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes around training and digits

ARITHMETIC

Inconsistent digit grouping breaks addition

Frontier LLMs measurably struggle with multi-digit arithmetic partly because digit chunking is not aligned to place value.

VERSION DRIFT

Vocabulary/model mismatch after a retrain

Token IDs from a newly trained vocabulary are meaningless to a model trained on the old one.

UNICODE

Byte-fallback edge cases

Rare scripts or malformed encodings can fall back to raw bytes mid-word, producing an unexpectedly long token run.

MERGE ORDER

Tie-break sensitivity across corpora

A slightly different training corpus, or a different tie-break rule, changes which pairs merge first — and therefore the final vocabulary.

FORMATTING

Commas and decimals fragment differently

“1,234,567” and “1234567” are not guaranteed to tokenize into comparable pieces just because the digits match.

COST BLIND SPOT

Treating vocabulary size as training-time-only cost

A large vocabulary also enlarges the embedding table and output softmax at every single inference call.

Misconceptions

Incorrect belief Correction

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Pin the exact tokenizer/vocabulary version to the exact model checkpoint; never assume a retrain is a safe drop-in.
  • Route arithmetic, counting, and date math to Oracle SQL or a calculator tool rather than trusting generated token-level digits.
  • Log token-to-character ratios by content type in production — code, numbers, and non-English text routinely deviate from prose.
  • Budget embedding-table and output-layer memory against vocabulary size, not just against expected sequence length.
  • Domain-specific tokenizer retraining is rare and expensive; consider it only after measuring genuine fragmentation-driven cost or quality loss.
  • Treat byte-level fallback as a safety net for representability, not as evidence that two different tokenizers agree on a split.

RECALL

Knowledge check

Why does merging (e, s) happen before merging (l, o) in the Section 06 trace?
Because (e, s) has a higher weighted frequency (9, from newest and widest) than (l, o) has at that same point (7, from low and lower) — BPE always merges the single most frequent pair first.
What is the practical difference between BPE and WordPiece merge scoring?
BPE merges whichever pair is simply most frequent; WordPiece merges the pair that most increases likelihood relative to how often its two parts occur independently, so a raw-frequency winner can lose to a rarer pair under WordPiece.
Why can “48213” and “482134” tokenize into different digit groupings?
Digit chunking was learned from whatever groupings recurred in the training corpus, not from place value or number length, so two similar numbers are not guaranteed comparable token boundaries.
Does tokenizer training ever run during a normal RAG request?
No — training happened once, offline, before the tokenizer was shipped; a request only applies the already-learned merge list.

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

Training vs. use

Training: offline, once, learns merges from frequency counts. Use: online, every request, applies the fixed merge list. BPE/WordPiece/SentencePiece-unigram differ only in the merge-scoring rule.

Core rule and use

  • score_BPE(a,b) = freq(a,b)
  • Use: reading and predicting how a term or number will fragment
  • Warning sign: trusting an LLM’s token-level digit math
  • Memorable example: “low/lower/newest/widest” → es, est, est·, lo, low
23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: What is the difference between tokenizer training and tokenizer use?
Training is an offline, one-time process that learns a merge list from corpus frequency counts; use is applying that fixed list to new text at every request, with no new learning involved.
Mathematical: How would you score a candidate BPE merge versus a candidate WordPiece merge?
BPE scores a candidate purely by its raw weighted pair frequency; WordPiece scores it by roughly how much more often the pair co-occurs than its two parts occur independently, which can favor a rarer pair over a more frequent one.
Coding: How would you implement the core loop of a BPE trainer?
Represent each word as a symbol sequence with a frequency; repeatedly count all adjacent pairs weighted by frequency, pick the maximum, replace every occurrence of that pair with a new merged symbol, and repeat until a target vocabulary size or merge count is reached.
Debugging: Users report a fine-tuned model suddenly producing garbled output. What tokenizer-related cause would you check first?
Whether the tokenizer or vocabulary version shipped with the fine-tune matches the one the base model was originally trained with — mismatched token IDs are silently meaningless to the model.
System design: A RAG assistant needs to report exact totals from Oracle metadata. Where does that calculation belong?
In an Oracle SQL query or a deterministic calculation step, not in the LLM’s generated text — digit tokenization is not aligned to place value, so token-level arithmetic is not reliable.
Production scenario: Your team wants to train a domain-specific tokenizer for heavily jargon-laden documents. What would you weigh first?
Whether general-vocabulary fragmentation is actually measurably hurting retrieval quality or token cost, since a new vocabulary requires retraining or careful re-alignment of any embedding or generation model that depends on it.
