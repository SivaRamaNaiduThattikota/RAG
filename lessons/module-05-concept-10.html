MODULE 05 · CONCEPT 10

Somebody labeled it —
here's how.

Concepts 08 and 09 built precision, recall, F1, MRR, MAP and nDCG entirely on top of one quiet assumption: that "relevant = {1, 2, 4, 7}" was simply handed to you. This concept is where that ground truth actually comes from. Real corpora are too large to judge exhaustively, so test collections get built by pooling — merging several systems' top-ranked results into one set humans actually review — and then checking whether two judges even agree with each other before trusting either one's labels.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

The label everyone else has been borrowing

CONCEPTS 08-09 — CONSUME

Precision, recall, F1, MRR, MAP and nDCG all start from the same sentence: "relevant = {1, 2, 4, 7}." Where that sentence comes from was never asked.

CONCEPT 10 — PRODUCE

A test collection (qrels): a fixed corpus, a fixed set of queries, and human relevance judgments over some — not all — of the (query, document) pairs between them.

First memory: "relevant" isn't a fact about a document — it's a label a human wrote down, and this concept is about how that label gets made.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Nobody can judge a million documents against a thousand queries

A corpus with 1 million documents and a query set of 1,000 topics has a billion possible (query, document) pairs. Exhaustively judging every one isn't a budget problem that scales down with more reviewers — it's simply infeasible at real scale. Something has to make judgment tractable without giving up on having ground truth at all.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Three systems, one pool, one document nobody found

Picture a tiny 50-document corpus and one query. Three systems each return their own fixed top-10 ranked list. Their union — the pool — is only 21 of the 50 documents: judges review just those 21, not all 50, and mark 7 of them relevant. But the corpus quietly hides an 8th relevant document, #44, that none of the three systems ever ranked in their top 10 — a judge who never sees a document can't ever mark it relevant.

THE POOL

21 of 50 documents judged, 42.0% coverage

Union of System A, B and C's top-10 lists — the only documents anyone ever looks at for this query.

THE ESCAPEE

Document #44 — relevant, never pooled

True total relevant = 8, but only 7 ever entered the pool. The 8th was structurally invisible to every system that built it.

A second, separate question: even inside the pool, do two judges agree with each other? Two judges reviewing the same 20 pooled pairs agree on the label 85% of the time raw — but once you correct for how often they'd agree by pure chance, that drops to a kappa of 0.68. Section 10 works both numbers in full.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Fix the corpus and queries, pool the rankings, judge only that

Fix the corpus — a stable, unchanging set of documents everyone evaluates against.
Fix the query set — a stable set of topics or questions, decided in advance.
Build the pool — union together several systems' top-k ranked results for each query.
Judge only the pool — humans label just those (query, document) pairs, binary or graded.
Assume the rest is negative — everything outside the pool is treated as not relevant, by convention, not by proof.
Optionally check the judges — a second judge relabels a sample; Cohen's kappa says whether they agree beyond chance.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Three distinctions worth keeping straight

TermIs NOTWhy it matters
Binary relevance {0,1}Graded relevance {0-3}MRR and MAP, as taught, need a strict yes/no flag. DCG and nDCG extend naturally to graded scales — that split, only previewed in Concept 09, is finished here.
A relevance judgmentPoolingA judgment is one human's label on one (query, document) pair. Pooling is the separate, earlier step of deciding which pairs get judged at all — merging several systems' top-k into one set humans actually look at.
Annotator agreement (kappa)CorrectnessKappa measures whether two judges land on the same label, corrected for chance — it says nothing about whether that shared label is actually true. High agreement can still be collectively wrong.
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Test collections are exhaustively judged."

False at real scale — pooling exists precisely because exhaustive judgment of every (query, document) pair is infeasible once a corpus gets large.

"An unjudged document is confirmed not relevant."

False — it's a working convention, not a verified fact. Document #44 in Section 03's example is genuinely relevant and simply never got the chance to be judged.

"One judge's opinion is simply ground truth."

False — a single judge's labels are only as trustworthy as they are, which is exactly what a second judge and Cohen's kappa are for checking.

"A system built after the original pool is evaluated fairly against old qrels."

False — pooling bias specifically penalizes any system that finds relevant documents no system in the original pool ever surfaced.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

Qrels = corpus + queries + judgments

A test collection is only useful because all three pieces are fixed together — change any one and it's a different qrel set.

Pooling = union of several systems' top-k, and it's necessary

Exhaustive judgment doesn't scale; pooling is the standard way to make judgment tractable at real corpus sizes.

Pooling bias = pool-based recall can overstate true recall

Whenever an escapee exists, the judged-relevant count is smaller than the true total, so the same numerator divided by a smaller denominator can only look better than it should.

Kappa = chance-corrected agreement, not raw percent agreement

Two judges can look like they agree 85% of the time and still only reach "substantial," not near-perfect, agreement once chance is subtracted out.

04
INTUITION

The food-critic circuit

No single critic can taste every dish at every restaurant in a city — exhaustive judging simply isn't possible. So review sites look at the union of dishes several critics already tried (pooling), and nobody fully trusts a rating until two critics roughly agree on it (annotator agreement). A dish no critic ever ordered stays unrated forever — not confirmed bad, just never tasted.

Analogy boundaryThis picture explains the shape of pooling and agreement, not their exact arithmetic — Section 05 and Section 09 give the precise definitions and the algebra the analogy alone can't supply.

09
MATHEMATICS

Why pooling can only overstate recall, never understate it

Pool coverage is simply the fraction of the corpus that ever gets judged: coverage = |P| / |corpus|. In Section 10's worked example that's 21/50 = 42.0% — the other 58% of the corpus is never looked at by anyone, for any query.

recall_pool = h / R_judged recall_true = h / R_true where R_judged ≤ R_true

For any system, h is the number of its own retrieved documents that also happen to be judged relevant — the same numerator in both fractions. Whenever the pool has at least one escapee, R_judged is strictly smaller than R_true, and dividing an unchanged numerator by a smaller denominator can only produce a value equal to or larger than the true fraction. Concretely, with h=4: 4/7 = 0.5714 ≥ 4/8 = 0.5000. Pool-based recall can only overstate true recall, or match it exactly when no escapee exists — it can never understate it.

Cohen's kappa follows the same Po/Pe/κ = (Po − Pe)/(1 − Pe) structure introduced in Section 05, with the numbers already computed in Section 06 and worked in full in Section 10; the Landis and Koch interpretation bands (κ around 0.61-0.80 read as "substantial agreement") are cited here as a common convention, not re-derived from anything more fundamental.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Every number here was independently verified before being written

Corpus of 50 documents, ids 1-50. Three systems each contribute a fixed top-10 ranked list for one query:

SystemTop-10 ranked list
A3, 7, 12, 15, 18, 22, 29, 31, 40, 45
B1, 7, 12, 16, 22, 25, 33, 38, 40, 47
C3, 9, 12, 19, 22, 27, 31, 36, 40, 49

Pool = A ∪ B ∪ C = 21 unique documents out of the 50-document corpus — 42.0% coverage. Judges review those 21 and mark 7 relevant: {7, 12, 22, 25, 31, 38, 49}. Ground truth (revealed only for teaching) has one more relevant document, #44, that none of the three systems ever ranked in their top 10 — true total relevant = 8.

System A's recallUsing judged-relevant count (7)Using true total (8)
A ∩ relevant hits = {7, 12, 22, 31} — 4 documents4 / 7 = 0.57144 / 8 = 0.5000

Pool-based recall overstates System A's true recall by (0.5714 − 0.5000) / 0.5000 = 14.3% relative — purely from trusting the pool's own relevant count as if it were the whole truth. Precision@10 for A = 4/10 = 0.40, unaffected by pooling since it only involves A's own already-judged retrievals.

Checking the judges: Cohen's kappa

Judge 2: relevantJudge 2: not relevant
Judge 1: relevant62
Judge 1: not relevant111

Observed agreement Po = (6+11)/20 = 0.85. Judge 1's marginal relevant rate = 8/20 = 0.40; Judge 2's = 7/20 = 0.35. Chance agreement Pe = 0.40×0.35 + 0.60×0.65 = 0.53. Kappa = (Po − Pe) / (1 − Pe) = (0.85 − 0.53) / (1 − 0.53) = 0.6809 — "substantial agreement" on the common Landis and Koch scale (0.61-0.80), cited here as a convention, not re-derived. Every number in this section was checked with a node script before being written.

CONCRETE USE

Four cards: two clean fits, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

TREC — the canonical pooled test collection

TREC's own tracks are the textbook example of building qrels by pooling several participating systems' top-ranked results, then having human assessors judge just that pool.

CLEAN FIT

An internal RAG golden set, built the same way

SMEs judging a pool assembled from BM25, dense and hybrid retriever configurations is the exact same pooling pattern from Section 10, just at a smaller, project-specific scale.

BOUNDARY CASE

A small, Cranfield-style corpus needs no pooling

When a corpus and query set are small enough, exhaustive judgment is actually feasible — pooling exists to solve a scale problem that a small enough collection simply doesn't have.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Scoring a new system against old, never-repooled qrels

A system built after the original pool was fixed gets penalized for surfacing relevant documents the pool never captured — the fix is re-pooling with the new system included, not trusting the old qrels forever.

CODE

Pool-building and Cohen's kappa, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
def build_pool(systems, k):
    pool = set()
    for ranked_list in systems:
        pool.update(ranked_list[:k])
    return pool

def cohens_kappa(both_yes, only_j1, only_j2, both_no):
    n = both_yes + only_j1 + only_j2 + both_no
    po = (both_yes + both_no) / n
    j1_yes = (both_yes + only_j1) / n
    j2_yes = (both_yes + only_j2) / n
    pe = j1_yes * j2_yes + (1 - j1_yes) * (1 - j2_yes)
    return (po - pe) / (1 - pe)

A = [3, 7, 12, 15, 18, 22, 29, 31, 40, 45]
B = [1, 7, 12, 16, 22, 25, 33, 38, 40, 47]
C = [3, 9, 12, 19, 22, 27, 31, 36, 40, 49]

pool = build_pool([A, B, C], k=10)
judged_relevant = {7, 12, 22, 25, 31, 38, 49}

hits_A = set(A) & judged_relevant
recall_pool_A = len(hits_A) / len(judged_relevant)
recall_true_A = len(hits_A) / 8  # true total, including escapee #44

kappa = cohens_kappa(both_yes=6, only_j1=2, only_j2=1, both_no=11)

assert len(pool) == 21
assert round(len(pool) / 50, 3) == 0.420
assert round(recall_pool_A, 4) == 0.5714
assert round(recall_true_A, 4) == 0.5000
assert round(kappa, 4) == 0.6809

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

POOLING BLIND SPOT

Penalizing a system for finding what pooling missed

A system that surfaces genuinely relevant documents no original pooling system ever ranked gets scored as if those documents don't exist — exactly the document #44 pattern from Section 10.

UNRELIABLE LABELS

Trusting judgments with low kappa

If two judges barely agree beyond chance, every downstream metric computed from either judge's labels inherits that unreliability, no matter how carefully precision or nDCG gets computed on top of it.

JUDGE FATIGUE

Judgment quality drifting over a long session

Judges reviewing hundreds of pairs in one sitting tend to get faster and less careful near the end — a real, practical source of noisy qrels that no formula can detect after the fact.

CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
Test collections are exhaustively labeledFalse at real scale — they're built by pooling. Only small collections (Cranfield-style) are ever fully judged end to end.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Judgment cost scales with pool size × query count — a real budget line, not a rounding error, once a project needs more than a handful of test queries.
  • Graded relevance costs meaningfully more to collect than binary relevance — fulfilling Concept 09's own forward reference to this trade-off.
  • Qrels need refreshing as new systems or new documents appear — an old pool judged before a system existed can't fairly evaluate it, per Section 11's mistake case.
  • Judge guidelines and training measurably raise kappa — annotator agreement isn't fixed; clearer instructions and calibration sessions genuinely move the number.
  • LLM-as-judge is named here only as a modern, cheaper proxy for human relevance labeling, forward-referenced to Module 18 — not taught in this concept.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why can pool-based recall never be lower than true recall, only equal to or higher?
Both fractions share the same numerator — the number of a system's own retrieved documents that are also judged relevant. The judged-relevant count in the denominator can only be smaller than or equal to the true total (some truly relevant documents may sit outside the pool as escapees), and dividing an unchanged numerator by a smaller-or-equal denominator can only produce an equal or larger result.
Why doesn't a high raw percent agreement between two judges guarantee reliable relevance judgments?
Raw agreement doesn't subtract out how often the two judges would agree purely by chance. Cohen's kappa corrects for that; two judges can post 85% raw agreement and still land at a kappa well below 1.0 once chance agreement is removed, as Section 10's own 0.6809 shows.
A brand-new retriever surfaces a genuinely relevant document that no pooling system ever ranked. What happens to how that document is scored?
It gets treated as not relevant by the unjudged convention, even though it's genuinely relevant — the new retriever is penalized for finding something the original pool structurally couldn't see, exactly the document #44 pattern from Section 10.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
Pool = D ∪ E = {2, 3, 5, 9, 10, 11, 14, 17} — 8 of 20 documents, 40% coverage. System D's own list is {2, 5, 9, 11, 14}; intersecting with the judged-relevant set {5, 9, 11, 17} gives {5, 9, 11} — 3 documents. Pool-based recall for D = 3/4 = 0.75 (the judged-relevant count is 4). True recall for D, using the real total of 5 (4 judged plus the escapee #20), = 3/5 = 0.60. That's a (0.75 − 0.60)/0.60 = 25.0% relative overstatement, purely from the pool missing #20. For the kappa table: Po = (9+11)/25 = 0.80. Judge 1's relevant rate = 12/25 = 0.48, Judge 2's = 11/25 = 0.44. Pe = 0.48×0.44 + 0.52×0.56 = 0.5024. Kappa = (0.80 − 0.5024)/(1 − 0.5024) = 0.598 — "moderate" agreement on the Landis-Koch scale, noticeably weaker than Section 10's own 0.6809.

Teach-back: explain, in your own words, why this exercise's recall inflation (25.0%) is larger than Section 10's own worked example (14.3%), even though both scenarios have exactly one escapee document.

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

Relevance judgments and test collections

  • Qrel: one human relevance label on one (query, document) pair — binary {0,1} or graded {0..3}
  • Pooling: union of several systems' top-k, judged instead of the whole corpus
  • Unjudged convention: outside the pool is assumed not relevant — an assumption, not a proof
  • Cohen's kappa: κ = (Po − Pe) / (1 − Pe) — chance-corrected agreement between two judges

Memorable example

3 systems, 50 documents: pool = 21 (42.0% coverage), 7 judged relevant, 1 escapee (#44) → System A's recall overstated 14.3% (0.5714 vs 0.5000). Two judges, 20 pairs: 85% raw agreement, κ = 0.6809.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Why is exhaustive relevance judgment infeasible at real corpus scale, and what does pooling trade away to fix that?
A large corpus times a real query set produces far more (query, document) pairs than any team can review. Pooling trades completeness for tractability — it judges only the union of several systems' top-k results, which means anything no pooled system ever ranked highly stays permanently unjudged.
A new retriever finds several genuinely relevant documents none of the original pooling systems ever surfaced. Why does it score artificially worse against the old qrels?
The unjudged convention treats anything outside the pool as not relevant. Since the new retriever's discoveries were never in the original pool, they're counted as false positives or simply ignored rather than credited as correct hits — the qrels themselves need re-pooling with the new system included before it can be evaluated fairly.
How would you explain Cohen's kappa vs. raw percent agreement to a non-technical stakeholder confused why "85% agreement" isn't good enough?
Raw percent agreement doesn't account for how often two judges would agree just by guessing, given how often each one tends to say "relevant" overall. Kappa subtracts that expected chance agreement out, so it answers a stricter question: how much better than random guessing are these two judges actually agreeing?
Design question: a new retriever configuration is about to ship. What would you do to your existing qrels before trusting an evaluation run against it?
Re-pool: run the new configuration alongside the existing systems, merge its top-k into the pool, and have judges label whatever's newly added before scoring the new system against the refreshed qrels — evaluating it against the stale pool would unfairly penalize anything it finds that the old pool never captured.
