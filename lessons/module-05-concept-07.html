MODULE 05 · CONCEPT 07

Longer isn't
more relevant.

Concept 06 wrote BM25's length-normalization parameter, b, straight into the formula — then held every document's length equal to the corpus average in every example, so b never once changed a score. This concept removes that simplification. A longer document naturally accumulates more term matches and a higher raw term frequency without necessarily being more relevant — it simply has more words for a term to land in. This concept derives the length-adjustment term L(d) = 1 − b + b × dl/avgdl that corrects for this, and works a complete example contrasting a genuinely short and a genuinely long document from the course's own five-ticket corpus, showing exactly how their BM25 scores shift once length is accounted for.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

L(d) stops being 1

BEFORE — CONCEPT 06

L(d) = 1 − b + b × dl/avgdl, but every example set dl = avgdl, so L(d) = 1 no matter what b was — b was in the formula, doing nothing.

NOW — CONCEPT 07

Real documents have real, different lengths. L(d) is computed from each document's actual length against the corpus average, and it genuinely raises or lowers that document's BM25 score.

First memory: nothing about BM25's formula changes in this concept — only the number that gets plugged in for L(d) stops being a placeholder.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

A document twice as long isn't twice as relevant

Take any document and paste an exact copy of itself onto the end. Every term's raw count doubles. Every term's tf doubles. Under Concept 05's raw tf-idf, and even under Concept 06's saturating BM25 with L(d) held at 1, that doubled document would score meaningfully higher for every term it contains — despite containing exactly zero new information. It's simply longer, and longer documents mechanically accumulate more term matches and higher counts almost for free. This concept exists to stop a document's score from rewarding length that isn't relevance.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

T2 (short) versus T3 (long), for real

Reusing the five-ticket corpus from Concepts 02–06: T1 has 9 words, T2 has 5, T3 has 10, T4 has 6, T5 has 6, so avgdl = (9+5+10+6+6)/5 = 7.2. T2 is shorter than average; T3 is longer than average. Using b = 0.75 (the Okapi default):

T2 — SHORT (dl=5)

L(T2) = 1 − 0.75 + 0.75×(5/7.2) = 0.25 + 0.5208 ≈ 0.7708 — below 1, because T2 is shorter than average.

T3 — LONG (dl=10)

L(T3) = 1 − 0.75 + 0.75×(10/7.2) = 0.25 + 1.0417 ≈ 1.2917 — above 1, because T3 is longer than average.

Plugging these into Concept 06's formula for the term "refund" (idf ≈ 0.9163, k1 = 1.2): without length normalization (L=1 for both, Concept 06's assumption), T2 scored 0.9163 and T3 scored 1.2599 — a gap of 0.3436. With real L(d) values, T2 rises to ≈1.0472 and T3 falls to ≈1.1357 — a gap of only ≈0.0885. T3 still edges ahead, because it genuinely repeats "refund" twice against T2's once, but most of its earlier advantage was simply because it's twice as long — and length normalization removes almost 75% of that gap.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

One term, plugged into a denominator you already know

THE FORMULA

L(d) = 1 − b + b × (dl / avgdl). dl is document d's own length; avgdl is the corpus's average length; b (0 to 1) controls how strongly length matters.

WHERE IT PLUGS IN

Unchanged from Concept 06: BM25(t,d) = idf(t) × [tf(t,d)(k1+1)] / [tf(t,d) + k1×L(d)]. A bigger L(d) makes the denominator bigger, which shrinks the saturating ratio — a longer-than-average document is penalized. A smaller L(d) shrinks the denominator, raising the ratio — a shorter-than-average document is rewarded.

WHAT b CONTROLS

b=0 makes L(d)=1 always (Concept 06's assumption, length ignored completely). b=1 makes L(d)=dl/avgdl exactly (length matters in full, linear proportion). b=0.75 (the usual default) blends the two.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"b=1 removes length normalization; b=0 applies it fully."

Backwards. b=0 makes L(d)=1 for every document, which is no length adjustment at all. b=1 applies length normalization in its strongest, fully linear form.

"Longer documents are always penalized by L(d)."

Only documents longer than the corpus average are penalized (L(d) > 1). A document shorter than average gets L(d) < 1, which actually boosts its score relative to Concept 06's fixed assumption.

"Length normalization guarantees the shorter document wins."

It doesn't — b03's T3 still narrowly outscores T2 after normalization, because T3 genuinely repeats the term more. Normalization corrects the unfair part of the gap; it doesn't erase genuine relevance differences.

"avgdl should be recomputed per query."

avgdl is a property of the corpus, computed once (and updated as documents are added or removed) — it does not depend on which query is being scored, unlike idf's df which is per-term but still corpus-wide, not per-query.

08 · YOUR PROJECT MAPPING

Where this lives in the Oracle RAG project

ORACLE TEXTTicket bodies vary wildly in length

A one-line ticket ("please refund my order") and a five-paragraph ticket describing the same issue should not be ranked apart purely because one is longer — length normalization is exactly the correction that keeps ranking fair across ticket lengths.

SUPPORT-TICKET SEARCHVerbose tickets stop gaming the ranking

Without length normalization, a customer who writes a long, rambling ticket that happens to repeat the query terms would rank higher purely from length. b03's numbers show almost 75% of that unfair advantage removed at b=0.75.

TEXT-TO-SQLLength is a ranking correction, not a filter

Filtering tickets by exact length ("show tickets under 50 words") is a deterministic SQL WHERE clause; L(d) is a soft scoring adjustment inside a relevance formula — this course keeps the two mechanisms distinct on purpose.

04
INTUITION

Judging essays of wildly different lengths

Imagine grading essays on how thoroughly they cover a topic, but one essay is one paragraph and another is ten pages. The ten-page essay will almost certainly mention the topic's key terms more times, purely from having ten times the space to do so — not necessarily because it covers the topic ten times as well. A fair grader adjusts for that: expecting proportionally more mentions from a longer essay before treating that repetition as extra evidence of quality, rather than crediting raw mention counts as if length didn't exist.

Analogy boundaryThe grading analogy explains why an adjustment is fair, but not the specific linear-blend shape L(d) uses, or why b sits between 0 and 1 rather than being fixed at one extreme — Section 09 derives that algebraically.

MECHANISM

Compute the ratio, blend it with b, plug it in

Compute dl/avgdl — T2: 5/7.2 ≈ 0.6944 (below 1, shorter than average). T3: 10/7.2 ≈ 1.3889 (above 1, longer than average).

Multiply by idf and sum across the query — exactly Concept 06's structure, now with a genuinely varying L(d) per document.

09
MATHEMATICS

Why L(d) always equals 1 at dl=avgdl, regardless of b

L(d) = (1 − b) + b × (dl / avgdl) If dl = avgdl: L(d) = (1 − b) + b × 1 = (1 − b) + b = 1

This is exactly why Concept 06's examples showed no effect from b: they fixed dl = avgdl in every case, and the two b terms cancel algebraically to exactly 1 regardless of what b was set to. Once dl ≠ avgdl, that cancellation no longer happens: for T3 (dl=10, avgdl=7.2), the ratio dl/avgdl ≈ 1.3889 is greater than 1, so the b×(dl/avgdl) term outweighs what b subtracted, and L(T3) > 1. For T2 (dl=5), the opposite happens, and L(T2) < 1. b controls how much this deviation from 1 is allowed to matter — at b=0, the deviation is multiplied by 0 and vanishes entirely, recovering Concept 06's assumption exactly.

11
CONCRETE USE

Two clean fits, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

b03/s10's short-versus-long comparison

The clearest possible demonstration: same term, same k1, only length differs, and the score gap visibly narrows.

CLEAN FIT

A padded, duplicated document

Pasting a document onto itself doubles both tf and dl; L(d) rises accordingly, blunting most (though not all) of the artificial score gain the padding would otherwise produce.

BOUNDARY CASE

dl = avgdl exactly

L(d) = 1 exactly regardless of b — a document of exactly average length behaves identically to Concept 06's simplified assumption.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Normalizing against a fixed constant instead of avgdl

Using a hardcoded "typical length" instead of the corpus's own avgdl breaks the moment the corpus composition changes — avgdl must be recomputed as documents are added or removed, the same operational point Concept 04 made about idf.

CODE

Full BM25 with real length normalization, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
import math

tickets = {
    "T1": "my order is late and my order never arrived",
    "T2": "please refund my order immediately",
    "T3": "the refund was processed but the refund amount is wrong",
    "T4": "where is my order confirmation email",
    "T5": "i need help resetting my password",
}

def doc_length(text):
    return len(text.split(" "))

AVGDL = sum(doc_length(t) for t in tickets.values()) / len(tickets)  # 7.2

def term_frequency(term, text):
    return text.split(" ").count(term)

def document_frequency(term, corpus):
    return sum(1 for text in corpus.values() if term in text.split(" "))

def inverse_document_frequency(term, corpus):
    df = document_frequency(term, corpus)
    if df == 0:
        return None
    return math.log(len(corpus) / df)

def length_term(doc_id, corpus, b):
    dl = doc_length(corpus[doc_id])
    return (1 - b) + b * (dl / AVGDL)

def bm25_term(term, doc_id, corpus, k1=1.2, b=0.75):
    idf = inverse_document_frequency(term, corpus)
    if idf is None:
        return 0.0
    tf = term_frequency(term, corpus[doc_id])
    L = length_term(doc_id, corpus, b)
    ratio = (tf * (k1 + 1)) / (tf + k1 * L)
    return idf * ratio

# b=0 must reproduce Concept 06's fixed L(d)=1 behavior exactly.
assert abs(length_term("T3", tickets, 0.0) - 1.0) < 1e-9

# With real b=0.75, T2 (short) should score higher than it did at L=1,
# and T3 (long) should score lower than it did at L=1.
score_t2 = bm25_term("refund", "T2", tickets, b=0.75)
score_t3 = bm25_term("refund", "T3", tickets, b=0.75)
score_t2_nolen = bm25_term("refund", "T2", tickets, b=0.0)
score_t3_nolen = bm25_term("refund", "T3", tickets, b=0.0)
assert score_t2 > score_t2_nolen
assert score_t3 < score_t3_nolen
assert abs(score_t2 - 1.0472) < 0.001
assert abs(score_t3 - 1.1357) < 0.001

# The gap between them shrinks once length is normalized.
assert (score_t3 - score_t2) < (score_t3_nolen - score_t2_nolen)
13
ECOSYSTEM

Where production systems apply this exact correction

Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, and Lucene's BM25Similarity all compute avgdl once per field and per shard, updating it as documents are indexed or removed, then apply this exact L(d) term with b defaulting to 0.75 — the identical default used throughout this concept. Both engines expose b as a per-field tuning knob because different content types normalize differently: log lines and short product titles often normalize length more aggressively (higher b) than long-form articles, where length differences carry more genuine signal about topic depth.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

MISTUNED b

b too low under-corrects for length

A low b leaves most of the length-driven advantage from Section 09/s10 intact, letting long, verbose documents keep outranking shorter, more precise ones for reasons unrelated to relevance.

MISTUNED b

b too high over-corrects for length

A very high b can penalize genuinely comprehensive long documents so heavily that legitimately thorough coverage of a topic gets suppressed — length and depth are correlated, not identical.

STALE avgdl

An outdated avgdl skews every score

If avgdl isn't recomputed after the corpus's length distribution shifts significantly (e.g. bulk-adding many long documents), every existing document's L(d) becomes wrong relative to the new average, silently distorting rankings corpus-wide.

17
CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
b=1 turns off length normalizationFalse — b=1 applies it in its strongest, fully linear form. b=0 is the one that turns it off.
Length normalization always favors shorter documentsFalse — a document exactly at avgdl is unaffected, and s10's T3 still wins after normalization; only the unfair portion of its advantage is removed.
k1 and b solve the same problem from two anglesFalse — k1 bounds how much repetition within one document can matter; b bounds how much a document's overall size can matter. Neither substitutes for the other.
18
REALITY

Production implications

  • avgdl must be maintained as a live, corpus-wide statistic, recomputed incrementally as documents are added, updated, or deleted — treating it as a one-time constant silently drifts every subsequent score's fairness over time.
  • b is almost always left at 0.75 unless a relevance-evaluation process (Concepts 08–10, and Module 18 later) shows a specific collection's length distribution benefits from a different value — content types with unusually wide length variance (forum posts mixed with long-form articles) are the most common case for retuning it.
  • Because L(d) depends only on a document's own length and the corpus-wide avgdl, it can be precomputed and cached per document at index time — no extra per-query cost beyond a single multiplication that Concept 06's formula already required.

RECALL

Knowledge check

Write L(d) and state what happens to it when dl=avgdl.
L(d) = 1 − b + b × dl/avgdl. When dl=avgdl, the ratio dl/avgdl=1, so L(d) = 1−b+b = 1, regardless of b's value.
Why did T2's BM25 score rise while T3's fell, once real lengths were used?
T2 (dl=5) is shorter than avgdl=7.2, so L(T2)<1, which shrinks the denominator and raises T2's saturating ratio. T3 (dl=10) is longer than avgdl, so L(T3)>1, which enlarges the denominator and lowers T3's saturating ratio.
What value of b exactly reproduces Concept 06's assumption that L(d)=1 for every document?
b=0 — at b=0, L(d) = 1−0+0×(dl/avgdl) = 1 for any document regardless of its actual length.

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
L(T4) = 1−0.75+0.75×(6/7.2) = 0.25+0.625 = 0.875. L(T1) = 1−0.75+0.75×(9/7.2) = 0.25+0.9375 = 1.1875. Document-length normalization

  • L(d) = 1 − b + b × dl/avgdl: compares a document's length to the corpus average, blended by b.
  • Plugs into Concept 06's denominator unchanged: BM25(t,d) = idf(t) × tf(k1+1)/(tf+k1·L(d)).
  • b=0: length ignored (Concept 06's assumption). b=1: full linear normalization. b=0.75: the usual default blend.
  • Why it's needed: longer documents mechanically accumulate more term matches without necessarily being more relevant.

Memorable example

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Explain document-length normalization to someone who already understands BM25's saturating tf factor, in under thirty seconds.
BM25's denominator also contains a term called L(d) that compares this document's length to the corpus's average length. If the document is longer than average, L(d) is greater than 1 and the score is pulled down a bit; if it's shorter, L(d) is less than 1 and the score is pulled up. A parameter b, from 0 to 1, controls how strongly that adjustment applies.
Why do longer documents need this correction at all?
A longer document mechanically has more room for any given term to appear in, so it naturally accumulates higher raw term frequency without necessarily being more topically relevant. Without normalization, that length advantage would be indistinguishable from genuine relevance.
A teammate sets b=0 "to keep things simple." What are they actually choosing?
They're choosing to disable length normalization entirely — L(d)=1 for every document regardless of its actual length, which is exactly Concept 06's simplifying assumption, not a neutral default.
Design question: a corpus mixes one-line chat messages with full incident reports. How would you expect the optimal b to differ from a corpus of uniformly-sized documents?
A corpus with wide length variance has more to gain from length normalization, since raw length differences are large and would otherwise dominate scores unfairly — such collections often benefit from a b closer to 1. A corpus where every document is close to avgdl already has L(d) close to 1 for everyone regardless of b, so the choice of b matters far less.
