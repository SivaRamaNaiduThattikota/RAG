MODULE 07 · CONCEPT 05

The highest-scoring chunk
can still be unreadable.

Concepts 02 through 04 each picked a splitting METHOD. None of them answered a question that applies to all three equally: once you've decided how to split, how big should each piece actually be? A chunk that's too small retrieves precisely but can leave a dangling reference with nothing to resolve it against. A chunk that's too large stays fully readable but dilutes its own embedding — Concept 01's dilution mechanism, recurring here as a size choice rather than a chunk/no-chunk decision — and wastes context on sentences a given query never needed.

PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

One dial, two ways to lose

Concepts 02-04 chose a splitting method. This concept asks the question that sits on top of all three: for whichever method you picked, how big should a chunk actually be? Shrink it and you risk losing context a reader would need. Grow it and you dilute the very relevance you were trying to preserve.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Chunk size is a dial, not a binary choice

THIS CONCEPT

Once a splitting method is chosen (Concepts 02-04), how big should the resulting chunks be? Small chunks retrieve precisely but risk losing context a reader needs. Large chunks stay readable but dilute relevance and waste budget on content a given query didn't ask for.

NOT THIS CONCEPT

Chunk overlap (Concept 06) and parent-document/small-to-big retrieval (Concept 07) — the two engineered mitigations for this exact trade-off, named forward only.

First memory: the small-chunk failure and the large-chunk failure are two ends of the SAME dial, not two separate problems — moving away from one always moves you toward the other.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 04 already named this question

Concept 04's own closing line asked it directly: "once a splitting method is chosen, how big should the resulting chunks actually be?" Every method from Concepts 02 through 04 can produce a chunk that's too small or too large, regardless of which one picked the boundary — fixed-size, recursive, sentence-aware, or semantic, the size question sits on top of all of them equally.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Meridian Freight's claims window, and a dangling "the window"

Five sentences about a shipment-insurance policy: (S1) "Meridian Freight offers two shipment-insurance tiers for commercial accounts: Basic and Priority." (S2) "Priority-tier accounts get an extended claims-filing window instead of Basic tier's standard rule." (S3) "The window is 60 days." (S4) "Once the window elapses without a filed claim, Meridian's system marks the shipment closed and no further compensation is issued." (S5) "Accounts enrolled in auto-renewal keep Priority-tier status without needing to resubmit paperwork each year." Query: "What happens if a claim isn't filed before the window elapses?" S4 alone answers the "what happens" half — but "the window" has no antecedent unless S3 comes with it.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Shrink loses context; grow dilutes relevance

DirectionWhat happensFailure mode
ShrinkFewer sentences per chunk, embedding narrows to one ideaAn antecedent or qualifier the sentence depends on can land in a different chunk
GrowMore sentences per chunk, embedding averages more ideas (Concept 01's pooling formula, reused directly)Relevance dilutes and unrelated content rides along, wasting context-window budget

Both directions are the same dial. There is no size that avoids both failure modes at once for every possible query — only a size that's appropriate for the antecedent chains this particular corpus actually has.

05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

A parameter of the method, not a fourth method

Concepts 02-04 — which splitting method↓Concept 05 — how big, a parameter of that method↓Concepts 06-07 — engineered mitigations

Size isn't a fifth splitting technique sitting alongside fixed-size, recursive, sentence-aware, and semantic — it's a parameter that applies to whichever of those four already made the boundary decision.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Bigger is always safer."

False — Chunk C shows zero extra comprehension gain over Chunk B, for a real further precision cost.

"There's one universal correct chunk size."

False — it depends on how long the antecedent chains in a given corpus actually run, which varies by document type.

"Smallest possible chunk is always best."

False — Chunk A scores highest yet is the one chunk in this passage that's actually unreadable on its own.

INTUITION

A camera zoom, not a simple more-or-less

Zoom a camera in tight on one subject and the shot is sharp and precise — but it crops out the surrounding scene that would explain what's actually happening. Zoom out to capture the whole scene and the subject you care about becomes a small, diluted fraction of the frame. Neither setting is wrong; each is the right choice for a different question.

06
MECHANISM

Two lanes: shrink loses context, grow dilutes and wastes

SHRINK LANE

Fewer sentences per chunk narrows the embedding to one idea — raises match precision, raises the risk that a needed antecedent lands in a different chunk entirely.

GROW LANE

More sentences per chunk averages more ideas via Concept 01's pooling formula — lowers precision, resolves more antecedents, but raises the fraction of retrieved content irrelevant to any one query.

ARCHITECTURE

09
MATHEMATICS

The same pooling formula, now varying chunk membership

e_chunk = (1/n) · Σ e_i (Concept 01's own average-pooling formula)
waste_fraction = (n − n_minimal) / n

Nothing new mathematically — the same pooling formula from Concept 01, applied here to compare what happens as n (the number of sentences pooled into one chunk) grows from 1 to 5 over the identical passage.

WORKED EXAMPLE

0.9946 → 0.7273 → 0.6497, and what each drop buys

ChunkSentencesCosine to queryAntecedent
A — Tight{S4}0.9946Unresolved — "the window" undefined
B — Minimal complete{S3, S4}0.7273Resolved
C — Whole passage{S1..S5}0.6497Resolved, 3 of 5 sentences (60%) unused for this query

A→B drop: 0.2673 absolute, 26.87% relative — buys a resolved antecedent. B→C drop: 0.0776 absolute, 10.67% relative — buys nothing further for this query. A→C total drop: 34.68% relative. Every figure was computed once via an executed Node.js script (dot/norm/cosine/average-pool helpers) and independently hand-recomputed from raw dot/norm arithmetic for chunks B and C, matching to 6 decimal places.

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean fit, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

FAQ-style factoid queries

The answer sentence is usually already self-contained — small chunks work fine, little antecedent risk.

BOUNDARY CASE

Clause-heavy legal or contract text

Several chained cross-references can need a larger minimal-sufficient unit than a single sentence provides.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

One fixed global chunk_size for a heterogeneous corpus

Different document types carry different antecedent-chain lengths — a size tuned for one can fail badly on another.

12
CODE

The three chunk variants, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
import math

def dot(a, b):
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))

def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    return dot(a, b) / (math.sqrt(dot(a, a)) * math.sqrt(dot(b, b)))

def average_pool(vectors):
    n = len(vectors)
    dims = len(vectors[0])
    return [sum(v[i] for v in vectors) / n for i in range(dims)]

S1 = [0.85, 0.10, 0.05]
S2 = [0.70, 0.65, 0.05]
S3 = [0.10, 0.90, 0.05]
S4 = [0.05, 0.15, 0.95]
S5 = [0.05, 0.20, 0.85]
Q  = [0.05, 0.05, 0.95]

chunk_a = average_pool([S4])
chunk_b = average_pool([S3, S4])
chunk_c = average_pool([S1, S2, S3, S4, S5])

cos_a = cosine_similarity(Q, chunk_a)
cos_b = cosine_similarity(Q, chunk_b)
cos_c = cosine_similarity(Q, chunk_c)

assert round(cos_a, 4) == 0.9946
assert round(cos_b, 4) == 0.7273
assert round(cos_c, 4) == 0.6497
assert cos_a > cos_b > cos_c  # precision falls monotonically as the chunk grows on this path
13
ECOSYSTEM

Sourced defaults from real chunking tools

Pinecone's own chunking-strategies guide states the narrow-vs-broad trade-off directly: smaller chunks (around 256 tokens) for granular semantic precision, larger chunks (512-1024 tokens) for retaining more context. LlamaIndex's SentenceSplitter defaults to DEFAULT_CHUNK_SIZE = 1024 tokens with a 20-token overlap (confirmed directly from source) — a real, currently-shipping default sitting in the broad band. LangChain's TextSplitter defaults chunk_size to 4000 characters (confirmed directly from source) — roughly 800-1000 tokens at typical English prose density, an important units caveat: chunk_size is sometimes measured in characters, sometimes in tokens, and confusing the two silently changes what "4000" actually means.

Failure modes

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • chunk_size affects embedding-call count and index storage directly — more, smaller chunks means more total chunks to embed and store.
  • Larger chunks consume more context-window budget at generation time per retrieved result, competing with Module 02 Concept 10's lost-in-the-middle risk.
  • chunk_size is normally tuned empirically per corpus against retrieval-quality metrics, not chosen once and left fixed — Module 18's evaluation material, named forward only, not taught here.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why does Chunk A score highest on the query yet fail to usefully answer it?
Cosine similarity measures how closely a chunk's embedding matches the query, not whether the chunk is self-contained. Chunk A's tight focus maximizes similarity but strips away the antecedent "the window" depends on — a high score and an unreadable chunk at the same time.
Why is the A-to-B drop "worth it" while the B-to-C drop is "wasted"?
The A-to-B drop buys something real: a resolved antecedent the query needs. The B-to-C drop buys nothing further for this specific query — three of the five sentences in Chunk C do no work answering it, so that additional precision cost has no offsetting benefit.
Why is chunk size independent of Concept 04's embedding/LLM-call cost axis?
Concept 04's cost axis is about which chunking METHOD is used (semantic and proposition chunking cost more calls than fixed-size or recursive). Size is about how much content goes into each chunk once a method is already chosen — a cheap method can still be sized well or badly, and an expensive method's chunks can still be too small or too large.

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

Chunk size as a precision-context trade-off

  • Shrink: raises precision, raises antecedent-loss risk
  • Grow: lowers antecedent-loss risk, dilutes relevance and wastes budget (Concept 01's pooling formula, recurring)
  • Core relation: one dial, two opposite failure modes — not two separate problems

Memorable example

A 1-sentence chunk scores highest (0.9946) yet is unreadable — "the window" has no antecedent. A 2-sentence chunk resolves it for 26.87% less precision. The whole 5-sentence passage pays a further 10.67% for zero extra comprehension, wasting 60% of its own sentences on this query.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why isn't the smallest possible chunk always the best choice?
A smaller chunk maximizes embedding precision to a query but risks stripping away context (an antecedent, a qualifier) the chunk needs to be understood on its own — Chunk A's own dangling "the window" is the concrete case.
Mathematical: derive how average pooling of n vectors affects cosine similarity to a fixed query as n grows.
Average pooling computes e_chunk = (1/n)·Σe_i. As unrelated or less-aligned vectors join the average, the resulting vector drifts toward the mean direction of all pooled vectors rather than staying aligned with any single one — cosine similarity to a fixed query generally falls as n grows, unless every newly-added vector happens to point in exactly the query's own direction.
Coding: sketch a minimal-sufficient-chunk finder (pseudocode is fine).
Starting from a single sentence, incrementally grow the chunk (adding the next sentence) while checking whether all pronouns/definite references within the current chunk have an antecedent inside that same chunk; stop growing as soon as that check passes, rather than committing to one fixed size upfront.
Debugging: a corpus's retrieval quality drops after doubling chunk_size. What do you check first?
Whether the larger chunks are diluting embeddings by pooling unrelated content together (Concept 01's mechanism) rather than genuinely capturing more useful context — measure average intra-chunk topic coherence before assuming size alone is the fix.
System design: how would you choose chunk_size differently for a FAQ corpus versus a legal-contract corpus?
A FAQ corpus's answers are typically self-contained per entry, favoring a narrow chunk_size close to one Q&A pair. A legal-contract corpus often has long cross-reference chains between clauses, favoring a broader chunk_size or a structural/hierarchical unit (Concepts 07-08, named forward) large enough to keep a clause's referents together.
