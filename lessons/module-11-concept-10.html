MODULE 11 · CONCEPT 10

A follow-up isn't a fresh query.
Rewriting turns before they retrieve, and paying for history once they're kept.

Concept 08 closed the online path with one answer to one question. Real conversations don't stop there -- the next turn is often "does that still apply if it's a gift," which shares almost no words with the chunk that actually answers it. This concept rewrites a follow-up against prior turns before retrieval ever runs, node-verifies exactly how much that changes which chunk ranks first, and then treats the growing conversation itself as a second thing -- alongside evidence -- competing for Concept 07's fixed prompt budget.

PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

Concept 08 answered one question. This concept is what happens on the second one.

Everything through Concept 08 handles exactly one query: embed it, retrieve, select, assemble, generate, cite. A real conversation doesn't hand the pipeline one query -- it hands it a sequence of turns, and most follow-up turns lean on words the user never actually says again ("that," "it," "still"). This concept is the step that rewrites a follow-up against what was already said, before retrieval runs on it, and then accounts for what keeping that conversation around actually costs.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

The second question was never asked in full

Turn 1 asks "what's the return window after the grace period." Turn 2 asks "does that still apply if it's a gift." Read on its own, Turn 2 has no subject -- "that" points nowhere without Turn 1 in view. Conversation history is the mechanism that lets a follow-up turn borrow the missing subject from prior turns, either by rewriting the query text itself before retrieval, or by feeding both the rewritten query and the prior exchanges into the same prompt Concept 07 assembles.

First memory: a follow-up query is elliptical on purpose -- humans don't repeat themselves -- so something has to fill in what it's actually asking before retrieval can find the right chunk.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Retrieval can't rank what it can't read

Concept 05's retrieval step embeds a query and ranks chunks by similarity to it. If that query is "does that still apply if it's a gift," there is no "that" in the vector space -- the embedding has nothing to anchor to, and retrieval either returns nothing useful or returns whatever happens to share a stray word. Without this concept, every follow-up after the first turn would retrieve close to randomly. Conversation history exists to fix the query itself before it ever reaches Concept 05, and separately, to track what's already been said so the model isn't re-told things or asked to contradict its own prior answer.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Three turns, one node-verified conversation

Turn 1: "what's the return window after the grace period" -- Concept 06/07's own established query. Turn 1's answer deliberately reuses Concept 08's lab scenario, not its worked example: "the order-cancellation grace period is 90 days" (Concept 08 flagged this as its lab's own illustrative answer, distinct from Section 07's "14 days"). Turn 2: "does that still apply if it's a gift" -- raw, this scores 0.000 against every one of the five evidence chunks under a lexical-overlap retrieval proxy; rewritten to "does the order-cancellation return window and grace period still apply if the order is a gift order," it scores 1.000 on c007's child, the correct chunk. Turn 3: "what about if I already opened the package" behaves less cleanly -- see Section 07.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Rewrite first, then retrieve; track cost the whole time

Two separate jobs share the name "conversation history." First: before retrieval, expand or rewrite the raw follow-up into a self-contained query using prior turns -- either a rule-based template or a small model call, verified here with a plain lexical-overlap scorer standing in for a real embedding comparison. Second: after rewriting, decide how much of the actual conversation transcript gets carried into the prompt itself, because every kept turn is tokens that compete with retrieved evidence for the same fixed budget Concept 07 defined.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Rewriting isn't retrieval, and history isn't evidence

Rewriting a query and retrieving with it are two different steps -- this concept only owns the first one; Concept 05 still does the actual ranking, unchanged. Conversation history is also not evidence: evidence comes from the corpus and gets cited; history is what the user and the model already said to each other in this session, and it can be wrong (Turn 1's own "90 days" is deliberately not the corrected figure) without ever being "un-cited," because nothing about a citation claim applies to a turn of chat.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Rewriting always finds the ideal chunk."

Not automatically -- Turn 3's own rewritten query still ranks the wrong chunk (cC, 0.176) ahead of the actually-relevant one (cA, 0.050), because the overlap scorer has no stemming and never matches "package" to "packaging." Rewriting fixes the zero-recall failure Turn 2 had; it doesn't guarantee an optimal ranking.

"Unbounded history is fine for a short conversation."

It is, right up until it isn't -- this exact 3-turn conversation only costs 74 tokens, but the same average turn size, left untrimmed, overflows the remaining history allowance by turn 107. Small conversations hide the problem; they don't remove it.

Readiness check before moving on

Explain why a raw follow-up can fail retrieval

It shares no vocabulary with the answering chunk -- Turn 2's raw query scores 0.000 against all five evidence chunks.

State what rewriting does and doesn't fix

It fixes zero-recall failures caused by missing referents (Turn 2); it doesn't guarantee the resulting ranking is optimal (Turn 3, due to the scorer's own stemming gap).

Do the budget arithmetic for history vs. evidence

This conversation's full lean history costs 74 of the 3,584-token budget; unbounded growth at this size overflows the remaining history allowance after turn 107.

JAVASCRIPT · OVERLAP SCORE + TOKEN-COUNT PROXY (node-run, see Section 09) 
function overlapScore(query, chunkText) {
  const qTerms = new Set(terms(query));       // lowercased, stopwords removed
  const cTerms = new Set(terms(chunkText));
  let shared = 0;
  for (const t of qTerms) if (cTerms.has(t)) shared++;
  return cTerms.size === 0 ? 0 : shared / cTerms.size;
}

WORKED EXAMPLE

Raw query vs. rewritten query, node-verified retrieval ranking

Same five-chunk evidence set as Concept 06/07 throughout: cA (a 30-day standard return policy), c007's child (the grace-period sentence), cB (shipping/tracking), cC (order-status values), cE (pre-shipment cancellation). Scored with the Section 06 overlap function, run via node against the exact turn text.

TurnQuery formRanking (chunk=score, descending)
2raw: "does that still apply if it's a gift"cA=0.000, c007_child=0.000, cB=0.000, cC=0.000, cE=0.000
rewritten: "does the order-cancellation return window and grace period still apply if the order is a gift order"c007_child=1.000, cC=0.235, cE=0.136, cB=0.045, cA=0.000

Turn 2 is the clean case: the raw query retrieves nothing at all -- every chunk scores exactly 0.000, because "does that still apply if it's a gift" shares zero non-stopword terms with any of the five chunks. Rewritten, it scores a perfect 1.000 against c007's child, the one chunk that actually contains "grace period" and "order cancellation." Rewriting turned a total retrieval failure into a perfect top match.

The honest complication -- Turn 3 doesn't clean up the same way: the raw Turn 3 query weakly favors cB (0.045, the shipping/tracking chunk -- wrong). Rewritten, the top chunk becomes cC (0.176, order-status values) -- also arguably not the best answer, since cA (the actual 30-day-return-with-original-packaging policy) only scores 0.050 and ranks last among the non-zero chunks. The reason: the overlap scorer has no stemming, so the query's word "package" never matches the chunk's word "packaging" -- a real, mechanical limitation of this proxy scorer, not a flaw in the rewriting step itself. Rewriting fixed Turn 2's zero-recall failure outright; for Turn 3 it only improved the ranking, and a real embedding model (which generalizes across "package"/"packaging" far better than exact term overlap) would very plausibly do better than this proxy shows.
08
WORKED EXAMPLE

History and evidence, competing for the same 3,584-token budget

Per-turn token counts, word-count proxy, node-computed: Turn 1 -- user 9, assistant 20, total 29. Turn 2 -- user 9, assistant 28, total 37. Turn 3 -- user 8 (its own live query has no assistant turn yet), total 8. History carried into Turn 3 (Turns 1+2, user+assistant) is 29+37 = 66 tokens; Turn 3's own rewritten query costs 15 tokens.

assembled(turn 3) = sys(35) + history(66) + evidence(880) + boundaries(30) + query(15) = 1,026 tokens prompt budget (Concept 07) = 3,584 tokens → 2,558 tokens of headroom history's share of the assembled prompt = 66 / 1,026 = 6.4% -- evidence's share = 880 / 1,026 = 85.8%

History is a small fraction of this particular assembled prompt -- but that's because this conversation is only three turns long. Holding sys/evidence/boundaries/query fixed at their Turn-3 values leaves 3,584 − 960 = 2,624 tokens of budget available to history alone. Simulating this conversation's own average turn size (24.7 tokens/turn) with no trimming at all, that 2,624-token allowance is exceeded after turn 107 (cumulative history at that point: 2,639.3 tokens). Trimmed with a sliding window instead, keeping the most recent 3 of this conversation's 3 turns costs exactly 74 tokens (29+37+8) -- comfortably inside the allowance, because this specific conversation never got long enough to need trimming yet. The 107-turn figure is what shows the mitigation actually matters at scale, not in this 3-turn example.

15
CONTRAST

Lean history policy vs. a naive full-snapshot policy

A naive implementation might store, as "history," the entire assembled prompt from each turn -- system instructions, all five evidence chunks, boundaries, and that turn's query -- instead of just the user/assistant exchange. Re-using Turn 3's own 1,026-token assembled total as that per-turn snapshot size, and the same 2,624-token allowance from Section 08:

PolicyPer-turn costTurns until the 2,624-token allowance overflows
Lean (user/assistant text only)~24.7 tokens/turn average, this conversationTurn 107 (cumulative 2,639.3 tokens)
Naive (re-snapshots the full assembled prompt every turn)1,026 tokens/turn (re-injects all 880 tokens of evidence, every turn)Turn 3 (cumulative 3,078 tokens)

Both policies are node-simulated against the identical allowance. The naive policy doesn't fail because its logic is wrong -- it fails because it pays the evidence cost a second time, every single turn, for evidence retrieval already has a place to live (Concepts 05-07's own retrieve-and-assemble step, run fresh each turn). Storing it again inside "history" is pure duplication, and duplication compounds.

Incorrect beliefCorrection

17
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why does a raw follow-up like "does that still apply if it's a gift" fail retrieval on its own?
Because retrieval ranks by vocabulary/embedding overlap with the query, and an elliptical follow-up's missing referent ("that") means the query itself carries none of the words or meaning needed to match the right chunk -- Turn 2's raw query scores 0.000 against every evidence chunk under the overlap proxy used here.
Conceptual: does query rewriting guarantee correct retrieval afterward?
No -- it fixes referent-missing failures (Turn 2's clean 0.000 → 1.000 case) but doesn't fix other gaps in the retrieval method itself, such as this proxy scorer's lack of stemming, which still misranks Turn 3 after rewriting.
System design: why does a naive "snapshot the full prompt into history" policy blow the budget so much faster than a lean one?
Because it re-stores the retrieved evidence a second time, every turn, on top of the evidence Concepts 05-07 already re-retrieve and re-assemble fresh each turn -- pure duplication that compounds turn over turn (turn 3 overflow vs. turn 107 for the lean policy, same allowance).

20
CROSS-REFERENCE

An unverified claim in history is exactly as risky as an unverified citation

Concept 08's Section 20 established that a [SOURCE n] tag is cheap to attach and has to be checked against the cited chunk's actual text before it's trusted. Turn 1's own assistant message in Section 04 is the same risk one turn earlier: it states "90 days" -- Concept 08's deliberately illustrative lab answer, not the corrected "14 days" figure its own Section 07 worked example established -- and that unchecked figure now sits in conversation history, available to be repeated or built on by a later turn. Nothing about carrying a turn forward into history re-verifies what that turn claimed; the same read-back discipline Concept 08 asked for on citations applies just as much to a prior turn's own unverified assertion.

21
CROSS-REFERENCE

Where history lands in the assembled prompt still matters

Module 02 Concept 10's lost-in-the-middle effect, already applied to evidence-chunk ordering in Concept 07 Section 21, applies just as much to conversation history once it's added to the same assembled string: history placed in the middle of a long prompt is recalled less reliably than history near the start or end, even when every token of it is intact. This concept doesn't resolve where history should sit in the string -- it only establishes that history is now one more thing inside that string, subject to the same positional risk evidence already carries.

22
CROSS-REFERENCE

Growth that compounds looks small right up until it doesn't

Module 09 Concept 07's 3.4x tenant-filter over-fetch fact and this concept's turn-107-vs-turn-3 contrast share the same shape: a cost that looks negligible at small scale (over-fetching a few extra candidates; carrying 74 tokens of history) can compound into the dominant cost once the input that drives it -- corpus size there, turn count here -- grows. Section 15's naive policy isn't a strawman for this reason: re-injecting the full evidence set into history every turn is the conversational equivalent of never dropping the over-fetched candidates that didn't survive filtering.

23
EVIDENCE + CONTROL

A fourth thing that can make an answer wrong

Module 03 Concept 11's three-link failure chain -- retrieval, augmentation, generation -- was framed around a single-turn query. In a multi-turn conversation, a wrong answer can now also trace back to a rewriting failure that happens strictly before all three: if Turn 2's raw query had been sent to retrieval unrewritten, the resulting 0.000-scored, effectively random evidence set would make the retrieval link look broken when the actual defect was upstream of it, in the rewrite step this concept owns. Checking whether the query was rewritten correctly, before checking retrieval, augmentation, or generation, is the one new diagnostic question multi-turn conversations add to that chain.
