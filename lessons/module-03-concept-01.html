01 · CONCEPT FIRST

A retriever wired to a generator — not a new model

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is an architecture pattern, not a model and not a training procedure. It's two components wired together: a retriever that searches an external corpus — a collection of documents that lives outside the model's weights — for passages relevant to a query, and a generator — Module 02's LLM, completely unchanged — that produces an answer conditioned on whatever the retriever handed it, instead of drawing only on its own parametric memory.

Conditioning the generator's output on retrieved evidence like this is called grounding. Grounding means the evidence was actually sitting in the prompt when the answer got written. It is not a correctness guarantee — the generator still has to read that evidence and use it well, and later concepts own every way that can go wrong.

First memory: RAG is retriever + generator, wired together — not a new model, not a new training run.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

The decode loop never checks its own memory

Module 02 Concept 09 already showed the shape of the problem: the decode loop has no verification step built in, and it answers from lossy, compressed weight-group residue left over from training — parametric memory, and nothing else. RAG is the architectural response to exactly that gap: insert an evidence-gathering step before generation ever runs, so the generator has something besides its own memory to read.

WHAT THIS SECTION COVERS

Just enough "why" to motivate the shape of the fix — an evidence step, inserted before generation.

WHAT IT DOESN'T COVER YET

Cost, knowledge cutoffs, private and constantly-changing knowledge, and why you can't just ask the LLM to fix this itself — that full case is Concept 02's job, next.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

One clause, two outcomes

Someone asks Oracle RAG: "Does our travel policy require pre-approval for international conferences?" That single question plays out two different ways, depending on whether a retriever is in the loop at all.

PARAMETRIC-ONLY (MODULE 02'S MECHANISM ALONE)

The generator answers straight from weight-group residue — the same mechanism Concept 09 walked through — fluently and confidently. It can also be flatly wrong about this specific internal policy, for exactly the reason Concept 09 already gave: nothing in that mechanism checks itself against the actual document.

RAG

The retriever searches the corpus, finds the actual policy passage covering international-travel pre-approval, and hands it to the generator. The generator reads that passage and answers from it. That's the whole story at this level: the retriever found the passage, the generator read it and answered.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Two boxes, each with exactly one job

RETRIEVER

Takes the query, searches the corpus, returns the top passages. How it searches — embeddings, vector indexes, similarity scoring — is deliberately out of scope until Modules 04 through 10 build it piece by piece. For now, treat it as a box with one job: find relevant text.

GENERATOR

The identical LLM mechanism from Module 02 — tokenize, forward pass, decode loop. Nothing about that mechanism is new here. Only its input changed: the prompt now contains retrieved passages plus the question, instead of the bare question alone.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often misunderstand

"RAG is a type of model."

No — it's an architecture pattern combining two existing, separately-understood components: a retriever and a generator.

"RAG means the model was retrained on your documents."

No — no training occurs. The corpus is searched live, at query time, not memorized into weights.

"Grounding guarantees a correct answer."

No — grounding means the output was conditioned on retrieved evidence, not that it's automatically correct. Failure modes are later concepts' job.

"Retrieval replaces the LLM."

No — retrieval only finds evidence. Generation is still required to actually produce the answer.

04
INTUITION

An open-book exam, not a closed-book one

Module 02's LLM alone is a closed-book exam — every answer drawn from memory, exactly Concept 09's world. RAG opens the book before the question gets answered: a research assistant (the retriever) finds the right pages in that book (the corpus) and hands them to the exam-taker (the generator) before the exam-taker writes a single word.

Analogy boundaryThe research assistant doesn't write the essay. Handing over the right page is not the same as writing the right answer — the exam-taker still has to read it and synthesize a response, and can still get it wrong with the right page in hand. Grounding is not automatic correctness. Guu et al.'s REALM paper traces this exact pattern back through language-model pretraining, years before "RAG" became its name — this is an architecture idea, not one paper's invention.

PRECISION

Formal definitions

This is Lewis et al.'s 2020 formulation — the paper that coined "RAG" and framed it exactly this way, as a retriever composed with a generator under one conditional-generation objective.

MATHEMATICS

Notation for two worlds, not yet the scoring inside either

Concept 09's world, parametric only:

P(answer | query)

This concept's world, conditioned on retrieved evidence:

P(answer | query, context), where context = Retriever(query, corpus)

That's as far as the notation goes today. The retrieval scoring function itself — similarity, ranking — is Module 04's job (mathematics for retrieval), and fusing or reranking multiple retrieved passages is Module 16's. This section only names the shape of the two conditional distributions; it derives neither.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A three-document corpus, scored by hand

Toy corpus, three one-sentence documents, and a query:

Query: "Do I need approval for international conference travel?"
D1: "Employees must get manager approval before booking international conference travel over $2,000."
D2: "Domestic travel under $500 does not require approval."
D3: "The office coffee machine is serviced every Tuesday."

Score each document by simple keyword-overlap counting against the query's four content words — approval, international, conference, travel — a deliberate stand-in for real embeddings and vector search, not the real retrieval mechanism:

KEYWORD OVERLAP — TOY RETRIEVAL SCORE
D1
4 / 4 words
D2
2 / 4 words
D3
0 / 4 words

D1 wins by hand-count, so it's the passage the retriever returns. Now watch what changes in the generator's answer. Parametric-only, per Concept 09's worked-example style, the generator might guess by analogy to the more familiar domestic-travel rule it half-remembers — something like "the usual $500 threshold applies" — which is wrong for this policy. Handed D1 inside its prompt, the generator answers from the actual sentence: manager approval is required for international conference travel over $2,000. Same generator, same mechanism — the only thing that changed is what was sitting in the prompt when it wrote the answer.

CONCRETE USE

Examples and counterexamples

GOOD FIT

Internal policy Q&A over a document corpus

Exactly Section 10's shape — a fixed, searchable corpus and questions that need one of its specific passages.

GOOD FIT

Support queries over a changing knowledge base

A knowledge base that gets edited constantly is exactly the case parametric memory can't keep up with.

BOUNDARY CASE

A purely creative-writing prompt

No external fact is needed, so retrieval adds no value here — Concept 12 covers when RAG should not be used, in depth.

COUNTEREXAMPLE

A plain keyword-search UI, no generation step

Finding documents and listing them is not RAG — there's no generator reading them and writing an answer.

COUNTEREXAMPLE

A fine-tuned-only model, no live corpus

Baking documents into weights at training time is a different pattern entirely — nothing is searched at query time.

CODE

The two boxes, as the smallest possible stubs

PYTHON · FROM-SCRATCH PSEUDOCODE 
corpus = [
    "Employees must get manager approval before booking "
    "international conference travel over $2,000.",
    "Domestic travel under $500 does not require approval.",
    "The office coffee machine is serviced every Tuesday.",
]

def retrieve(query, corpus):
    # naive word-overlap scoring -- a stand-in for real embeddings
    # and vector search, which is Module 08's job, not this one.
    query_words = set(query.lower().split())
    scored = [
        (doc, len(query_words & set(doc.lower().split())))
        for doc in corpus
    ]
    scored = [pair for pair in scored if pair[1] > 0]
    scored.sort(key=lambda pair: pair[1], reverse=True)
    return [doc for doc, score in scored]

def generate(query, passages):
    # formats a prompt string only -- does not run a real decode
    # loop, which is Module 02's already-built machinery, cited
    # here rather than reimplemented.
    if not passages:
        return f"No evidence found in the corpus for: {query}"
    evidence = "\n".join(passages)
    return f"Evidence:\n{evidence}\n\nQuestion: {query}\nAnswer:"

passages = retrieve("approval for international conference travel", corpus)
print("retrieved:", passages[0])
print(generate("approval for international conference travel", passages))
print(generate("what is the office wifi password", retrieve("wifi password", corpus)))

16
DIAGNOSIS

Getting the pattern itself wrong

RETRIEVAL MISTAKEN FOR RAG

No generation step at all

A system that only finds and lists passages, with nothing reading and answering from them, is missing the half of the pattern that actually produces an answer.

FINE-TUNING MISTAKEN FOR RAG

No live corpus at all

Baking documents into weights at training time skips the query-time search step entirely — a different pattern, not a variant of this one.

GROUNDING MISTAKEN FOR CORRECTNESS

Section 04's boundary, again

Conditioning on retrieved evidence is not the same as verifying the answer against it — that gap is the deep failure chain Concept 11 owns.

GENERATOR ASSUMED TO NEED CHANGES

It doesn't

Nothing about the generator is modified or retrained to "participate" in RAG — only its prompt's contents change.

REALITY

Production implications, at introductory depth

  • The retriever and generator are two independently deployable, scalable, versionable components — each can be swapped or upgraded without touching the other.
  • Module 19's operations depth covers exactly how that independence gets exploited in production; it's flagged here, not delivered.
  • Most of the rest of Module 03, and the modules after it, exist specifically to make each of these two boxes trustworthy, measurable, and secure — none of that starts until this pattern is locked in first.

RECALL

Knowledge check

Define RAG's two parts from memory.
A retriever that searches an external corpus for relevant passages, and a generator — an LLM, unmodified — that answers conditioned on whatever the retriever found.
What does grounding guarantee, and what does it not guarantee?
It guarantees the generator's output was conditioned on retrieved evidence at generation time. It does not guarantee the answer is factually correct.
Name all four vocabulary terms with one-line definitions.
Retriever: searches a corpus for relevant passages. Corpus: the external documents being searched. Generator: the LLM producing the answer, unchanged from Module 02. Grounding: conditioning generation on retrieved evidence.
Does a retrieval-only system count as RAG? Does a fine-tuning-only system?
Neither does. Retrieval-only has no generation/grounding step, so no answer ever gets written from the evidence. Fine-tuning-only has no live corpus — knowledge gets baked into weights instead of searched at query time. Both are missing one required half of the pattern.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Explain RAG to a non-technical stakeholder in one sentence.
It's a search step wired in front of the AI's answer, so it reads your actual documents before it responds, instead of relying only on what it happened to learn during training.
A candidate says RAG fine-tunes the model on your documents. Correct them.
No training happens in RAG. The documents live in a searchable corpus and get retrieved live, at query time; fine-tuning is a separate technique that bakes knowledge into weights instead, with none of the query-time search step.
What is the minimal set of components required before you can call a system RAG?
A corpus to search, a retriever that searches it and returns passages, and a generator that reads those passages and writes an answer conditioned on them. Drop any one of the three and it stops being RAG.
Sketch the two boxes of a RAG system for a support-ticket corpus, and name what each box owns.
Retriever box: owns finding the most relevant past tickets or knowledge-base articles for the incoming query. Generator box: owns reading whatever the retriever found and writing the actual reply — the retriever never writes anything, and the generator never searches anything.
