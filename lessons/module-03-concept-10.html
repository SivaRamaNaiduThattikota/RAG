MODULE 03 · CONCEPT 10

Knowledge-intensive tasks
and answerability.

Concepts 02 and 03 argued, in general, why an LLM alone cannot be trusted with company-specific or fast-changing facts. Concept 08 then asked whether RAG is the right architecture for a task at all. This concept asks a narrower, sharper question that sits before both: for one specific question against one specific corpus, is an answer even possible — no matter which architecture retrieves for it? Two independent reasons can say no. First, the task may not be knowledge-intensive at all — a request like "write a haiku" needs no fact lookup, so no retrieval, however excellent, could help it. Second, the task may be knowledge-intensive but the fact genuinely is not in the corpus — a coverage gap, not a retrieval bug. Confirming answerability first is what keeps Concept 11's failure chain honest: it only makes sense to ask where a pipeline broke once you already know the question could have been answered at all.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Two different questions, one gate before both

KNOWLEDGE-INTENSIVE

A task whose correct answer depends on a specific fact that lives outside the model's general ability and outside whatever the requester already supplied — a number, a date, a policy line, a name. If no outside fact is needed, retrieval has nothing to contribute, no matter how good it is.

ANSWERABLE (FOR THIS QUESTION, THIS CORPUS)

A knowledge-intensive question is answerable only if the specific corpus being retrieved from actually contains the fact it needs. A perfect retriever over the wrong corpus still can't produce a fact that was never there.

FAILURE REASON 1

The task simply isn't knowledge-intensive — "write a haiku," "translate this phrase." No corpus, however complete, changes the answer.

FAILURE REASON 2

The task is knowledge-intensive, but the fact genuinely isn't in the corpus — a coverage gap. This is a fact about the corpus, not a flaw in how retrieval searched it.

First memory: before blaming retrieval, ranking, or generation for a bad answer, ask whether the question could ever have been answered given what the corpus actually contains — two independent reasons can say no, and neither one is a pipeline bug.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Diagnosing before debugging

Concepts 02 and 03 already argued, in general, why an LLM's built-in knowledge isn't enough for company-specific or fast-changing facts. That argument explains why RAG exists at all — it doesn't tell you whether one particular question, against one particular corpus, can actually be answered. Skip that check and a wrong or missing answer looks like a pipeline failure every time, even when the real cause is that nobody could have answered the question with this corpus, or the question never needed a corpus in the first place. Concept 11's failure chain — which stage broke: chunking, embedding, retrieval, ranking, generation — only makes sense once you already know the question was answerable in principle.

WITHOUT THE ANSWERABILITY CHECK

Every wrong or missing answer triggers the same reflex: re-tune retrieval, re-chunk documents, swap the reranker — even when no pipeline change could ever have produced the fact.

WITH THE ANSWERABILITY CHECK

A wrong or missing answer first gets sorted: not knowledge-intensive, a coverage gap, or genuinely a pipeline problem — and only the third case belongs to Concept 11.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Three questions, one small Oracle HR corpus

Suppose the Oracle assistant's entire corpus is three short policy documents: a remote-work policy, a parental-leave policy, and an expense-reimbursement policy. Nothing else has been ingested yet.

REMOTE-WORK POLICY

Employees may work remotely up to 3 days per week with manager approval.

PARENTAL-LEAVE POLICY

12 weeks of paid leave are available after 1 year of employment.

EXPENSE-REIMBURSEMENT POLICY

Travel expenses must be submitted within 30 days, with receipts.

Three questions arrive. Each one fails, or succeeds, for a different reason:

"Write a two-line note congratulating a promoted employee."

Not knowledge-intensive — no policy fact is needed to write this at all. Unanswerable by retrieval, but not because of the corpus.

"How many remote workdays per week are employees allowed?"

Knowledge-intensive, and the remote-work document states it directly: 3 days. Answerable.

"Does the company offer unlimited PTO?"

Knowledge-intensive, but none of the three documents mentions PTO limits at all. A coverage gap — unanswerable with this corpus, no matter how well it's searched.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

The two-question test

QUESTION 1

Is this knowledge-intensive?

Does answering correctly require a specific outside fact — not something the model already reliably knows in general, not something the requester already supplied? If no, stop here: unanswerable by any retrieval, for reasons that have nothing to do with the corpus.

QUESTION 2

Is that fact in the corpus?

Only asked if question 1 is yes. Does the specific corpus you have actually contain the fact, anywhere, in any document? If no, it's a coverage gap: unanswerable with this corpus, though it might become answerable if the corpus grows.

BOTH YES

Then, and only then, it's a fair test

Knowledge-intensive and covered — the question is answerable in principle. Whether one specific pipeline run actually finds and uses that fact is Concept 11's separate question.

05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

A gate before the skeleton runs, not a box inside it

Query↓Answerability check (this concept)↓Retrieval↓Context↓LLM↓Answer

This check doesn't replace or reorder any of Concept 06's nine boxes — it's a question asked about the query and the corpus before the pipeline runs at all. A "yes" on both questions lets the query proceed into retrieval exactly as before. A "no" on either one means running the rest of the pipeline can't produce a correct, grounded answer — the right response is to route elsewhere (direct generation, for a non-knowledge-intensive task) or to flag the gap (for a coverage gap), not to push a doomed query further down the line.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"If RAG gives a wrong or missing answer, the pipeline is broken."

Not necessarily — check answerability first. Two of the three outcomes in Section 04's test mean the pipeline was never going to succeed, regardless of how well it ran.

"Knowledge-intensive means difficult."

It means fact-dependent, not hard. "What is 2 + 2" needs no outside fact and isn't knowledge-intensive; "what did our Q3 policy memo say" is knowledge-intensive even if the memo is short and simple.

"A creative-sounding request never needs the corpus."

Watch for an embedded factual constraint — "write a haiku that states our exact PTO cap" is knowledge-intensive despite its creative framing, because the number has to come from somewhere real.

"If the top-k results don't contain the fact, it's automatically a coverage gap."

Not automatically — the fact might be in a different document that just wasn't retrieved this time. That's a retrieval bug, Concept 11's territory, not a coverage gap; confirming the fact is truly absent from the whole corpus is what actually proves a coverage gap.

04
INTUITION

A reference librarian, asked three different kinds of question

Ask a reference librarian to write you a poem, and no amount of searching the shelves helps — the request never needed a book in the first place. Ask a different question — "what does the book on shelf 12 say about topic X" — and if the library genuinely never acquired that book, searching harder changes nothing either; the fact simply isn't on any shelf. Only the third case is a fair test of the librarian's search skill: a question that needs a fact, from a book the library actually owns.

Analogy boundaryA library has shelves and acquisition budgets that a RAG corpus doesn't share in the same way. The point of the analogy is the structural claim only — no fact needed, fact needed but absent, fact needed and present — not anything about physical libraries or librarians.
05
PRECISION

Formal definitions

TermDefinitionBoundary
Knowledge-intensive taskA task whose correct completion depends on a specific fact external to the model's general ability and to whatever the requester already supplied in the promptNot a claim about difficulty — a short, simple fact still makes a task knowledge-intensive
Corpus coverageA corpus covers a fact if that fact appears, explicitly or by direct inference, in at least one document actually ingested into the corpusCoverage is a property of the corpus's contents, independent of whether any particular retrieval run actually surfaces the fact
Answerable (question, corpus)A question is answerable against a corpus if it is knowledge-intensive and the corpus covers every fact it depends on — or if it is not knowledge-intensive at all, in which case the corpus is irrelevant to itAnswerability says an answer is possible in principle; it makes no claim about whether one specific pipeline run will actually produce it

The term "knowledge-intensive" itself is not new to this concept — Lewis, Perez, Piktus et al. (2020) used "knowledge-intensive NLP tasks" to describe exactly the category RAG was built for; Section 13 cites it directly for this framing rather than for coining "RAG," as Concept 01 did.

09
MATHEMATICS

A boolean predicate, not a new derivation

This concept stays at the diagnostic level, but its test is precise enough to write as a small boolean formula. Let K(q) be 1 if question q is knowledge-intensive and 0 otherwise, and let Covers(C, q) be 1 if corpus C contains every fact q depends on. Then:

Answerable(q, C) = ¬K(q) ∨ (K(q) ∧ Covers(C, q))

Read left to right: a question is answerable either because it needed no fact at all (the first term), or because it needed a fact and the corpus has it (the second term). The two terms are exactly Section 04's two failure reasons, negated: ¬K(q) false and Covers(C, q) false are the only two ways the whole expression comes out false. Nothing here measures how well a retriever ranks candidates — that's a separate, later question about a system's behavior, not about the (question, corpus) pair's underlying possibility.

11
CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, a counterexample, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

"How many remote workdays per week?" against the shown corpus

Knowledge-intensive, and the fact is stated directly — a fair test of retrieval, ranking, and generation, exactly as Concept 11 will assume.

BOUNDARY CASE

A fact stated only by implication, not by an exact sentence

If the remote-work document said "employees work from the office two days a week" without ever saying "three days remote," does the corpus cover "how many remote days"? By this concept's definition, yes — direct inference counts as coverage, not only an exact quoted sentence.

COUNTEREXAMPLE

"Write a haiku about our exact PTO cap."

Sounds creative, but the requested haiku has to contain a real number — the request is knowledge-intensive despite its creative framing, and subject to the same coverage check as any other fact-dependent question.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Trusting the model's own guess as proof of coverage

If the model answers a PTO question confidently from pretrained knowledge, that isn't evidence the corpus covers it — it's a sign the model may be answering from memory instead of the corpus, a hallucination risk this concept doesn't resolve, only flags.

12
CODE

The answerability check, from scratch

PYTHON · CLASSIFY A (QUESTION, CORPUS) PAIR AS ANSWERABLE OR NOT, AND WHY 
def check_answerability(is_knowledge_intensive, fact_in_corpus):
    # Section 09's boolean test, spelled out as branches instead of
    # a single expression -- the branch taken doubles as the reason.
    if not is_knowledge_intensive:
        return "not_knowledge_intensive"
    if not fact_in_corpus:
        return "coverage_gap"
    return "answerable"


# The five s10 rows, as boolean facts about each question
questions = [
    dict(is_knowledge_intensive=False, fact_in_corpus=None),  # congratulations note
    dict(is_knowledge_intensive=True,  fact_in_corpus=True),  # remote workdays
    dict(is_knowledge_intensive=True,  fact_in_corpus=False), # unlimited PTO
    dict(is_knowledge_intensive=True,  fact_in_corpus=True),  # parental leave
    dict(is_knowledge_intensive=False, fact_in_corpus=None),  # translation
]

for q in questions:
    print(check_answerability(q["is_knowledge_intensive"], q["fact_in_corpus"]))
# not_knowledge_intensive
# answerable
# coverage_gap
# answerable
# not_knowledge_intensive

assert check_answerability(False, None) == "not_knowledge_intensive"
assert check_answerability(True, False) == "coverage_gap"
assert check_answerability(True, True) == "answerable"
13
ECOSYSTEM

Where "knowledge-intensive" and "unanswerable" come from

Lewis, Perez, Piktus et al. (2020) frame RAG itself around "knowledge-intensive NLP tasks" — Section 05 reuses their term directly, this time for its diagnostic content rather than for coining RAG's name, as Concept 01 used it. Petroni, Piktus, Fan et al. (2020), KILT, turns "knowledge-intensive" into a benchmark category with eleven separate tasks, all sharing the same property this concept isolates: correctness depends on an external knowledge source, not on the model alone. Rajpurkar, Jia & Liang (2018) built the second reason this concept names into a benchmark of its own — SQuAD 2.0 deliberately added questions with no answer in the passage at all, so that a system's ability to recognize a coverage gap, not just retrieve well, could be measured directly.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes — of the answerability check itself

MISSED FACT

Calling a request "creative" without checking for an embedded fact

Section 11's counterexample is the risk here — a request can look purely generative while still depending on a real number or name buried inside it.

UNVERIFIED COVERAGE

Assuming the corpus covers a fact without checking

"Covers" means the fact is actually present in an ingested document — assuming it must be there because the topic seems related isn't verification.

MISDIAGNOSED GAP

Calling a retrieval bug a coverage gap

If the fact is genuinely present somewhere in the corpus but a specific run's top-k missed it, that's Concept 11's territory — confirm absence from the whole corpus before naming a coverage gap.

SKIPPED GATE

Jumping straight to Concept 11's failure chain

The failure chain presupposes answerability — running it on an unanswerable question wastes debugging effort on a question no pipeline change could have fixed.

CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
A wrong answer always means the pipeline is brokenTwo of Section 07's three outcomes mean the question was never answerable in the first place — no pipeline change would have fixed it.
"Knowledge-intensive" is a synonym for "hard question"It names fact-dependency, not difficulty — a one-word factual lookup is knowledge-intensive; a long creative-writing task usually isn't.
A coverage gap and a retrieval bug are the same problem with different namesA coverage gap means the fact was never ingested; a retrieval bug means it was ingested but a specific run failed to surface it — different causes, different fixes.
18
REALITY

Production implications, at introductory depth

  • Log confirmed coverage gaps as an ingestion signal — a recurring gap points at a real document the corpus is missing, feeding Module 6's ingestion work rather than a retrieval re-tune.
  • Confirmed unanswerable questions — both kinds — make useful negative examples for the evaluation sets Module 18 builds, distinct from questions that are answerable but were answered wrong.
  • Treat this check as a triage step, not a one-time audit — new questions arrive continuously, and a corpus that covered yesterday's questions may not cover tomorrow's.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

What are the two independent reasons a question can be unanswerable, and how are they different?
Not knowledge-intensive (no outside fact needed at all) and a coverage gap (a fact is needed but genuinely absent from the corpus) — the first makes the corpus irrelevant, the second is a fact about what the corpus is missing.
Why isn't "the top-k results didn't contain the fact" proof of a coverage gap?
The fact might exist in a different document that this particular run simply failed to retrieve — that's a retrieval bug, Concept 11's question, not proof the fact is absent from the whole corpus.
Why must this concept's check run before Concept 11's failure chain, not after?
The failure chain diagnoses where in the pipeline an answerable question went wrong — it presupposes an answer was possible, which is exactly the fact this concept's two-question test establishes first.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open one defensible reasoning pattern
Question 1: yes, a specific rate is a fact, so this is knowledge-intensive. Question 2: the only document that could plausibly cover it — expense-reimbursement — says nothing about a per diem rate, so the fact is absent from the whole corpus as shown. Outcome: a coverage gap, not a retrieval bug, because there's no rate anywhere to retrieve. The one additional fact that would change the outcome: ingesting a document that actually states a per diem rate would flip this to answerable.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

A user reports that a RAG system "hallucinated" an answer to a question your corpus never covers. Walk through how you'd confirm whether this is a coverage gap before touching the retrieval or generation code.
Confirm the question is knowledge-intensive, then search the full corpus — not just one run's top-k — for the fact. If it's genuinely absent everywhere, it's a coverage gap and the fix is ingestion, not a retrieval or prompt change; if the fact exists elsewhere in the corpus, it's a retrieval bug instead.
How would you explain to a product manager why "just add more re-ranking" won't fix every bad answer?
Re-ranking can only reorder candidates that retrieval already found among the corpus's actual contents — it can't manufacture a fact the corpus never had, and it adds nothing when the task never needed a fact in the first place.
Design question: how would you build a lightweight, ongoing signal for coverage gaps in a live system, without building a full evaluation pipeline?
Log knowledge-intensive queries where the system abstains or where user feedback flags a wrong answer, then periodically check whether the underlying fact is confirmed absent from the whole corpus — a recurring absent-fact pattern is a strong ingestion-backlog signal even without a formal eval suite.
