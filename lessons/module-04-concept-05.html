MODULE 04 · CONCEPT 05

Probability and
conditional probability.

Concepts 01 through 04 built one pillar of this module's mathematics: a vector's own norm, then two vectors compared by direction and by distance. This concept opens the second pillar, probability, from a clean start. A discrete probability distribution assigns a number to every possible outcome so that all of them add up to exactly 1. Conditional probability, P(A|B), asks a sharper question: given that B has already happened, how likely is A now? Bayes' rule is the one identity that flips a conditional probability around — from P(evidence | hypothesis), which is often easy to estimate, to P(hypothesis | evidence), which is usually what you actually want to know. Turning a raw retrieval score into an actual probability distribution is Concept 06's job, not this one.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

A probability distribution, and conditional probability — numbers that must add up to 1

DISCRETE PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTION

Every possible outcome gets a number between 0 and 1, and all of those numbers add up to exactly 1. A fair six-sided die: P(1) = … = P(6) = 1/6 each, and 6 × (1/6) = 1.

CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

P(A|B) asks: given that B already happened, how likely is A now? P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B) — not, in general, the same number as P(A) alone.

NOT A SIMILARITY SCORE

Concept 03's cosine similarity and Concept 04's distance each describe a relationship between two given vectors — neither one sums to 1 across anything. A probability distribution, by definition, must.

BAYES' RULE

The one identity that flips a conditional probability around: P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B). Derived in full in the advanced section, from nothing but the line above.

A CLEAN START

This concept reuses none of Concepts 01-04's vector mechanics. Its only inputs are outcomes, events, and counts.

NOT YET: TURNING SCORES INTO PROBABILITIES

Converting an arbitrary list of raw scores into an actual probability distribution is Concept 06's job (softmax) — not today's.

First memory: a probability distribution's numbers must add up to 1 across every outcome; conditional probability P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) asks about A once B is already known; Bayes' rule, P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B), is the one formula that flips that condition around.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Knowing an overall rate is not the same as knowing the rate given new evidence

An overall rate answers "on average, across everything." A conditional probability answers a sharper question: given one specific new piece of information, what should the estimate become right now? Without a formal way to update, the two get blurred together — either the overall rate gets used even after real evidence arrives, or the evidence gets used to jump straight to near-certainty, skipping the actual arithmetic.

WITHOUT CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

Across an entire ticket queue, only 25% of tickets turn out to be about refunds. Told nothing else about a new ticket, 25% is the best estimate available.

WITH CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

Told that a new ticket's text contains the word "refund," the right estimate is no longer 25% — it should update to reflect that specific evidence. Conditional probability is the tool that produces the updated number, not a guess.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

Twenty support tickets, one keyword, and an update worth computing

Twenty tickets sit in a queue. Five are actually about refunds; fifteen are not. Among the five refund tickets, four contain the literal word "refund" somewhere in the text (one customer wrote "money back" instead). Among the fifteen non-refund tickets, three happen to contain the word "refund" anyway — mentioning a past refund inside an unrelated shipping question.

OVERALL RATE (PRIOR)

P(Refund) = 5/20 = 0.25, before reading any ticket's text.

EVIDENCE, GIVEN THE CATEGORY

P("refund" | Refund) = 4/5 = 0.8.

EVIDENCE, GIVEN NOT THE CATEGORY

P("refund" | ¬Refund) = 3/15 = 0.2.

UPDATED RATE (POSTERIOR)

P(Refund | "refund") = 4/7 ≈ 0.571 — computed in full in b04 and Sections 09-10.

The word's presence pushed the estimate from 0.25 up to about 0.571 — more than double, but nowhere near 1.0, because plenty of non-refund tickets contain that same word too.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Two counting rules and one division

DISCRETE DISTRIBUTION

Sums to 1, across every outcome

For a complete, non-overlapping list of outcomes, each probability is ≥ 0 and the whole list adds to exactly 1 — never more, never less.

JOINT PROBABILITY

Both events at once

P(A∩B) = count(A and B both true) / total count — how often two conditions hold together, out of everything.

CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY

Divide the joint by the given event

P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B), defined only when P(B) > 0 — there's no valid estimate given something that never happens.

BAYES' RULE, NAMED HERE

The flip, proved in Section 09

P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B) — the full derivation uses nothing but the line above, written both ways.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"P(A|B) and P(B|A) are basically the same number."

Not in general — the prosecutor's fallacy. b03's own numbers: P("refund"|Refund) = 0.8, but P(Refund|"refund") ≈ 0.571. They only coincide when P(A) happens to equal P(B), a coincidence, not a rule.

"A cosine similarity of 0.8 means an 80% probability of relevance."

A similarity score is not a probability just because it's a number between -1 and 1 or 0 and 1 — it has no requirement to sum to 1 across any set of outcomes. Turning a score into a genuine probability is Concept 06's job.

"Conditional probability requires that B causes A."

It only requires statistical association within the data at hand. b03's word and category are correlated with no claim that the word causes the ticket's category.

"A probability distribution can have one outcome above 1, as long as others are small."

No — every individual probability is bounded in [0, 1], and the total across all outcomes is always exactly 1, regardless of how the mass is split up.

04
INTUITION

Updating an estimate once new evidence arrives

Picture a city where 30% of days are rainy overall — a plain marginal probability, no conditioning on anything yet. Now add one piece of evidence: the sky is heavily overcast this morning. Does the 30% figure for "rain today" still apply, unchanged? Almost certainly not — overcast mornings are more common on days that go on to rain, so the right estimate, once that specific evidence is known, is some genuinely different number. Conditional probability is the formal machinery for computing that updated number, instead of guessing at it.

Analogy boundaryWeather only illustrates that new evidence should change an estimate — it says nothing about the exact update, which the ticket example (b03, Sections 09-10) computes precisely, unlike a weather forecast.

06
MECHANISM

Tracing P(Refund | "refund") step by step

Fix the sample space — 20 tickets total, each one exactly one outcome.
Count the event "Refund" — 5 of 20 tickets. P(Refund) = 5/20 = 0.25.
Count the joint event "Refund AND 'refund'" — 4 of 20 tickets. P(Refund∩"refund") = 4/20 = 0.20.
Count the marginal event "'refund'" — 4 (from Refund) + 3 (from ¬Refund) = 7 of 20 tickets. P("refund") = 7/20 = 0.35.
Divide — P(Refund | "refund") = P(Refund∩"refund") / P("refund") = 0.20 / 0.35 = 4/7 ≈ 0.571.
07
VISUALIZATION

Prior in, evidence in, posterior out

Prior P(H)e.g. P(Refund) = 0.25
Likelihoods P(E|H), P(E|¬H)0.8 and 0.2
Total probability P(E)weighted sum = 0.35
Bayes' divisionP(E|H)P(H) / P(E)
Posterior P(H|E)≈ 0.571

Each stage strictly depends on the one before it — the posterior cannot be computed without first combining the prior and both likelihoods into a single total-evidence probability, P(E). Skipping straight from a likelihood to a posterior, without that middle step, is exactly the prosecutor's fallacy named in Section 16.

09
MATHEMATICS

Deriving Bayes' rule from the definition alone

For events A, B with P(A), P(B) > 0:

P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B) (definition, Section 05) P(B|A) = P(A∩B) / P(A) (the same definition, roles swapped) ⇒ P(A∩B) = P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) ⇒ P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B) -- Bayes' rule P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|¬A)P(¬A) -- law of total probability ⇒ P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / [P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|¬A)P(¬A)]

The first two lines are the same conditional-probability definition, applied once to P(A|B) and once to P(B|A) — nothing new yet. The third line notices that both give a formula for the same joint probability, P(A∩B), so those two formulas must be equal to each other. Rearranging that equality produces Bayes' rule directly: no additional assumption, no new axiom — just the same definition read twice. The fourth line, the law of total probability, expands the denominator P(B) into two pieces using the fact that A and ¬A partition every outcome; it lets the full posterior be computed from three numbers that are usually all that's ever measured directly: a prior, P(A), and two likelihoods, P(B|A) and P(B|¬A).

WORKED EXAMPLE

The full contingency table, two ways, agreeing

QuantityValueHow
P(Refund) — prior0.255 refund tickets / 20 total
P("refund" | Refund) — likelihood0.804 of the 5 refund tickets contain the word
P("refund" | ¬Refund) — likelihood0.203 of the 15 non-refund tickets contain the word
P("refund") — total evidence0.350.80×0.25 + 0.20×0.75, law of total probability (Section 09)
P(Refund | "refund") — posterior, via Bayes4/7 ≈ 0.571(0.80×0.25) / 0.35

The last two rows are the same number reached two independent ways — dividing through Bayes' rule symbolically, and counting the raw contingency table directly. They agree exactly, which is the whole point of Section 09's derivation: Bayes' rule is not a separate fact to trust on faith, it is a rearrangement of counting that must always match direct counting when both are done correctly.

11
CONCRETE USE

Four cards: a clean fit, a boundary case, an independent pair, and a mistake

CLEAN FIT

The refund-ticket update

P(Refund) = 0.25 updates to P(Refund | "refund") ≈ 0.571 — a real, checkable dependency between a word and a category (Section 10).

BOUNDARY CASE

An event that never occurs

If no ticket in the queue contains the nonsense string "xyzzy," then P("xyzzy") = 0 and P(Refund | "xyzzy") is undefined — there is no valid estimate conditioned on something impossible (Section 05).

INDEPENDENT PAIR

Shipping category and the word "invoice"

Of 20 tickets, 8 are "Shipping." The word "invoice" appears in 4 of those 8 Shipping tickets and in 6 of the 12 non-Shipping tickets — 50% either way. P("invoice") = 0.5, so P(Shipping | "invoice") = (0.5×0.4)/0.5 = 0.4, exactly P(Shipping) unchanged. Learning about the word did nothing here — true independence.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

The prosecutor's fallacy

Reading P("refund" | Refund) = 0.8 as if it answered "how likely is Refund, given the word?" The actual answer is 0.571 (Section 10) — a materially different number, from swapping which event is "given."

CODE

A contingency table, conditional probability, and Bayes' rule, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
# counts[category][has_word] -- Section 10's contingency table
counts = {
    "refund":     {"word": 4, "no_word": 1},   # 5 refund tickets
    "not_refund": {"word": 3, "no_word": 12},  # 15 non-refund tickets
}
total = sum(counts[c][w] for c in counts for w in counts[c])  # 20

def marginal(category):
    # Section 05 -- P(A) = count(A) / total
    return sum(counts[category].values()) / total

def joint(category, word_state):
    # Section 05 -- P(A ∩ B) = count(A and B) / total
    return counts[category][word_state] / total

def conditional(category, word_state):
    # Section 05 -- P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
    p_word_state = sum(counts[c][word_state] for c in counts) / total
    if p_word_state == 0:
        raise ValueError("cannot condition on an event with probability 0")
    return joint(category, word_state) / p_word_state

def bayes_rule(prior, likelihood_given_h, likelihood_given_not_h):
    # Section 09 -- P(H|E) = P(E|H)P(H) / [P(E|H)P(H) + P(E|notH)P(not H)]
    p_evidence = likelihood_given_h * prior + likelihood_given_not_h * (1 - prior)
    if p_evidence == 0:
        raise ValueError("cannot condition on an event with probability 0")
    return (likelihood_given_h * prior) / p_evidence


p_refund = marginal("refund")                                  # 0.25
p_word_given_refund = counts["refund"]["word"] / sum(counts["refund"].values())        # 0.8
p_word_given_not_refund = counts["not_refund"]["word"] / sum(counts["not_refund"].values())  # 0.2

direct = conditional("refund", "word")                         # counting method
via_bayes = bayes_rule(p_refund, p_word_given_refund, p_word_given_not_refund)  # formula method

print(round(direct, 3), round(via_bayes, 3))                   # 0.571 0.571
assert abs(direct - via_bayes) < 1e-9                           # Section 10's two methods agree

# independence check (Section 11's Shipping / "invoice" pair)
p_shipping = 8 / 20
p_invoice_given_shipping, p_invoice_given_not_shipping = 4 / 8, 6 / 12
p_shipping_given_invoice = bayes_rule(p_shipping, p_invoice_given_shipping, p_invoice_given_not_shipping)
assert abs(p_shipping_given_invoice - p_shipping) < 1e-9         # evidence changed nothing

# boundary case: conditioning on an impossible event
try:
    conditional("refund", "never_seen")
    assert False, "should have raised"
except KeyError:
    pass  # "never_seen" isn't even a tracked word state here -- a different, earlier failure than P(B)=0

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes — of computing and using conditional probability

PROSECUTOR'S FALLACY

Swapping P(A|B) for P(B|A)

Section 11's mistake to avoid, stated as a failure mode: treating a likelihood as if it were the posterior skips Section 09's division entirely and can overstate the true probability by a wide margin.

BASE RATE FALLACY

Ignoring a small prior in favor of a strong-sounding likelihood

A high P(E|H) feels convincing on its own, but if P(H) is tiny, Section 09's law of total probability can still leave P(H|E) surprisingly small — the prior never drops out of the computation.

UNSTABLE ESTIMATES FROM SMALL COUNTS

P(B) estimated from very few examples

A denominator built from a handful of observations is noisy, and if it happens to hit exactly 0, Section 05's undefined case is triggered outright, not just made unreliable.

SCORE TREATED AS A PROBABILITY

Feeding a raw similarity or distance score directly into a decision as if it already summed to 1

Concepts 03-04's numbers were never built to satisfy Section 05's distribution requirement — Concept 06's conversion step is what makes that legitimate, and skipping it is not.

CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
P(A|B) and P(B|A) are interchangeableThey are generally different numbers; they coincide only when P(A) happens to equal P(B) exactly — a coincidence, not something to expect (Section 11).
Independence can be assumed unless proven otherwiseIndependence is a specific numeric property, P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B); it must be checked, or deliberately designed in, never assumed by default (Section 05, Section 11).
A conditional probability describes causationIt only describes statistical association within the data at hand — Section 11's word-and-category pair are correlated with no claim that one causes the other.

REALITY

Production implications, at introductory depth

  • Real systems estimate likelihoods like P(E|H) from finite historical data, so a rare hypothesis or an unusual evidence combination can make P(E) extremely small — Section 16's instability, and part of why Concept 07's logarithms (forward reference) become necessary for numerically stable computation.
  • Naive Bayes classifiers (Section 13) work by assuming conditional independence between features given the class — an assumption that's often literally false in practice but still produces usable, reasonably well-ranked results.
  • A model's predicted probability should be checked for calibration — does "70% confident" actually correspond to being correct about 70% of the time on similar cases. This concept only introduces the probability itself; evaluating whether it's trustworthy is Module 18 (forward reference).

RECALL

Knowledge check

State the formula for conditional probability P(A|B), and the condition under which it's undefined.
P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B), undefined when P(B) = 0.
Using Section 10's numbers, is P(Refund | "refund") the same number as P("refund" | Refund)? Why does the difference matter?
No — 0.571 versus 0.8. Treating them as the same is the prosecutor's fallacy (Section 11, Section 16): it substitutes a likelihood for a posterior and can overstate how confident an estimate should actually be.
What single condition must a set of numbers satisfy to qualify as a discrete probability distribution?
Every value is ≥ 0, and the full set of values sums to exactly 1 across a complete, non-overlapping list of outcomes (Section 05).

Hands-on exercise

A different queue holds 25 emails: 10 are spam, 15 are not. Among the 10 spam emails, 9 contain a link. Among the 15 non-spam emails, 3 contain a link. Compute P(spam) as a prior, P(link|spam) and P(link|¬spam) as likelihoods, P(link) using the law of total probability, and finally P(spam|link) using Bayes' rule. Then check your answer by counting directly: how many of the emails that contain a link are actually spam, out of all the emails that contain a link at all?

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open one defensible answer
P(spam) = 10/25 = 0.4. P(link|spam) = 9/10 = 0.9. P(link|¬spam) = 3/15 = 0.2. P(link) = 0.9×0.4 + 0.2×0.6 = 0.36 + 0.12 = 0.48. P(spam|link) = (0.9×0.4)/0.48 = 0.36/0.48 = 0.75. Direct count: 9 spam-and-link emails out of 12 total link-containing emails (9 + 3) = 9/12 = 0.75 — the two methods agree exactly, the same check Section 10 ran on the ticket example.

Teach-back: explain, in your own words, why P(spam|link) = 0.75 is so much higher than the 0.4 prior, while P(link|spam) = 0.9 is a different number still, even though both numbers come from the exact same 25 emails.

PORTABLE MEMORY

Concept card

Probability and conditional probability

  • Distribution: P(ω) ≥ 0 for every outcome, Σ P(ω) = 1
  • Conditional: P(A|B) = P(A∩B) / P(B), for P(B) > 0
  • Bayes' rule: P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B)
  • Total probability: P(B) = P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|¬A)P(¬A)

Memorable example

25% of tickets are about refunds. Learning that a ticket contains the word "refund" updates that estimate to about 57%, not to 80% — the likelihood P("refund"|Refund) and the posterior P(Refund|"refund") are different numbers, and mixing them up is the prosecutor's fallacy.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Explain, conceptually, why a high P(evidence | hypothesis) does not automatically imply a high P(hypothesis | evidence).
The two describe different conditionals, and Bayes' rule shows the posterior also depends on the prior P(hypothesis) and on how often the evidence occurs overall, P(evidence). A rare hypothesis with a strong-sounding likelihood can still have a modest posterior once the prior and the total evidence probability are both accounted for — the base rate fallacy (Section 16).
Derive Bayes' rule starting from nothing but the definition of conditional probability.
P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) and P(B|A) = P(A∩B)/P(A) both define the same joint probability, so P(A|B)P(B) = P(B|A)P(A). Dividing both sides by P(B) gives P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B) directly (Section 09) — no assumption beyond the original definition.
Design question: a team wants to estimate P(document is relevant | retrieval score above threshold) but only has historical data for P(retrieval score above threshold | document is relevant). What else do they need, and how would they combine it?
They need the prior probability that a document is relevant at all, P(relevant), and the likelihood of a high score given an irrelevant document, P(score above threshold | ¬relevant) — the law of total probability (Section 09) combines all three into P(relevant | score above threshold) via Bayes' rule. Without the prior and the second likelihood, the single number they already have cannot be turned into the posterior they actually want.
