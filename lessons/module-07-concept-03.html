MODULE 07 · CONCEPT 03

"Contact Dr. Smith."
Where does that sentence actually end?

Concept 02's recursive method prefers a separator character over a raw one, but it still doesn't know what a sentence is — a 200-character run with no line break gets cut exactly like anything else. This concept moves past characters entirely into the document's real grammatical and structural units: abbreviation-aware sentence segmentation that refuses to treat "Dr." as a full stop, blank-line paragraph boundaries, and heading-scoped structural sections that group a heading with everything under it. The same passage, read three different ways, produces three different chunk counts — and no size parameter is involved anywhere.

PROGRESSIVE BEGINNER CORE

Reading the document instead of counting its characters

Fixed-size and recursive chunking (Concept 02) are both purely mechanical — neither one reads a sentence or a paragraph as an actual unit. This concept gives the first three unit types that do: sentences, paragraphs, and structural regions.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Sentences, paragraphs and sections — not separator characters

THIS CONCEPT

Splitting along the document's actual grammatical and structural units — sentence segmentation that checks a candidate period against an abbreviation exception before trusting it as a boundary, paragraph boundaries at blank lines, and structural boundaries at headings, tags or section breaks (reusing Module 06 Concept 02's format parsing directly, not re-deriving it).

NOT THIS CONCEPT

Embedding-based semantic or proposition grouping (Concept 04), the chunk-size trade-off (Concept 05), and chunk overlap (Concept 06) — all named forward only.

First memory: "Dr. Smith" is one abbreviation away from breaking a naive splitter in half. Abbreviation-aware segmentation fixes exactly that — it still isn't the same thing as understanding what the sentence means, which stays Concept 04's job.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concept 02 already admitted its own limit

Concept 02's own distinction table said it plainly: recursive chunking doesn't know what a sentence is. A single 200-character sentence with no internal line break would still get raw-cut by recursive chunking, exactly like fixed-size — separator preference only helps when a separator happens to be nearby. This concept closes that gap directly, not with a bigger separator list, but with three unit types recursive never had access to at all: sentences, paragraphs, and structural regions.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

"Contact Dr. Smith" — the whole problem in one line

Split naively on every period and "Contact Dr. Smith in the billing department if your claim is denied." produces "Contact Dr" as its own fragment — not a sentence by any definition, and not useful to embed or retrieve on its own. That one false split, caused by a single ordinary abbreviation, is the entire motivation for everything else in this concept.

Three unit types, three different signals

TechniqueHow it decides where to cutReuses

Structural boundaries are the one technique here that can't run on raw text alone — they need a document that's already been parsed into headings, tags or sections, which is exactly what Module 06 Concept 02 produced.

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"Splitting on every '.' is sentence splitting."

False — the "Contact Dr" proof. A period only marks a sentence boundary once it's been checked against likely abbreviations; treating every period as a boundary produces fragments that aren't sentences at all.

"Paragraph splitting understands document structure."

False — it's blind to headings specifically. It isolates "## Premium Plan Exception" as its own one-line orphan block, disconnected from the content it's actually introducing.

"Structural chunking is 'smarter' about meaning."

False — it's still boundary detection driven by markup (a heading tag, a nesting level), not by understanding what the section is about. That upgrade is Concept 04's job, not this one's.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

Abbreviation-aware vs. naive period-splitting

They diverge exactly at abbreviations — "Dr." is the memorable case, but any listed abbreviation triggers the same divergence.

Paragraph boundaries are blind to heading semantics

A blank-line splitter treats a heading line as just another block; structural boundaries do not.

Recursive's separator preference is still not linguistic awareness

Even when Concept 02's recursive method lands near a sentence-like boundary, that's coincidence from separator characters correlating with sentence ends — not grammar.

Concept 04 groups by meaning, not by any formatting signal at all — not covered here.

INTUITION

Someone reading with a pen, not scissors on a timer

Concept 02's methods were a pair of blind scissors on a timer and someone hunting for the nearest seam — both still working from characters alone. This concept is a person reading with a pen: they circle a period only once they've checked it isn't following "Dr", "e.g." or "U.S." — an ordinary abbreviation, not a sentence's end. 06
MECHANISM

Two lanes: a period check, and a heading-nesting rule

SENTENCE LANE

For each candidate period: look back to the immediately preceding contiguous word. If that word is in the abbreviation set, skip — the period is not a boundary. Otherwise, close the sentence here and start the next one right after it.

STRUCTURAL LANE

Split the document into blank-line blocks first. Any block that opens with a heading marker starts a new section that absorbs every following block until the next heading of equal or higher level appears — a heading is never left isolated the way paragraph splitting leaves it.

09
MATHEMATICS

No closed form here either — but one honest count and one rule

false_split_count = periods_total − true_sentence_count
(worked example: 4 − 3 = 1, located exactly at "Dr.")
level(section_i) ≤ level(section_i+1) unless a new heading opens a fresh section

Just like Concept 02's recursive method, sentence and structural boundary counts depend entirely on the passage's own content — there's no formula that predicts them in advance. What this concept can state precisely is how many boundaries naive splitting gets wrong (the false-split count above) and the nesting rule a structural splitter must respect: a section stays open until a heading of its own level or higher closes it.

11
CONCRETE USE

A clean fit, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

Well-formatted Markdown or HTML with clear headings

Structural chunking is essentially free and unambiguous when the source markup is well-formed — the heading levels do the work.

BOUNDARY CASE

Abbreviation-dense legal or technical text, or OCR-noisy scans

Documents packed with abbreviations like "Fig.", "Sec.", "U.S.C." stress the exception list hard; OCR-mangled periods from Module 06 Concept 04's noisy text can silently degrade sentence segmentation quality the same way a missing abbreviation would.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Treating structural chunking as if it already understood meaning

It only understands markup — a heading tag, a nesting level. Actual meaning-based grouping is Concept 04's distinct mechanism, not a bigger version of this one.

12
CODE

Both boundary detectors, from scratch

PYTHON · NO LIBRARIES 
import re

ABBREVIATIONS = {"Dr", "Mr", "Mrs", "Ms", "Prof", "Sr", "Jr"}

def split_sentences(text, abbrev_aware=True):
    sentences, start = [], 0
    for i, ch in enumerate(text):
        if ch != ".":
            continue
        j = i - 1
        while j >= 0 and text[j].isalpha():
            j -= 1
        word = text[j + 1:i]
        is_abbrev = abbrev_aware and word in ABBREVIATIONS
        if not is_abbrev:
            sentences.append(text[start:i + 1].strip())
            start = i + 1
    rest = text[start:].strip()
    if rest:
        sentences.append(rest)
    return sentences

def structural_split(text):
    blocks = text.split("\n\n")
    sections, cur = [], None
    for block in blocks:
        if re.match(r"^#{1,6}\s", block):
            if cur is not None:
                sections.append(cur)
            cur = block
        else:
            cur = block if cur is None else cur + "\n\n" + block
    if cur is not None:
        sections.append(cur)
    return sections

S1 = "Refund requests must be submitted within 45 days of purchase."
S2 = "Contact Dr. Smith in the billing department if your claim is denied."
S3 = "Employees on the premium plan receive an extended 90 day window for filing a claim."
PARA1, PARA2 = S1 + " " + S2, S3
TEXT2 = "\n\n".join(["# Refund Policy", PARA1, "## Premium Plan Exception", PARA2])

sentences = split_sentences(PARA1) + split_sentences(PARA2)
paragraphs = TEXT2.split("\n\n")
sections = structural_split(TEXT2)

assert len(TEXT2) == 259
assert len(sentences) == 3
assert len(paragraphs) == 4
assert len(sections) == 2
assert len(split_sentences(PARA1, abbrev_aware=False)) == 3  # "Contact Dr." false split

ECOSYSTEM

The same two lanes, in real tooling

For the linguistic half: NLTK's PunktSentenceTokenizer learns its abbreviation model unsupervised from a corpus rather than from a fixed list, and spaCy's sentence segmentation (its rule-based Sentencizer, or the dependency-parse-driven senter in a full pipeline) is the production alternative used inside a larger NLP stack. For the structural half: LangChain's MarkdownHeaderTextSplitter implements exactly the heading-scoped grouping taught here, directly reusing Module 06 Concept 02's Markdown heading-level distinction rather than re-deriving it. One honest limit: this concept's from-scratch exception list is a fixed set of strings — it has no way to weigh context the way Punkt's statistical model does, which matters most exactly where an abbreviation can legitimately sit at the end of a sentence (Section 20's transfer exercise runs straight into this).

CONTRAST

Sentence vs. paragraph vs. structural vs. recursive

MethodStrengthWeakness
SentenceEvery chunk is a complete grammatical thought — no "Contact Dr" fragmentsNeeds an abbreviation list or statistical model; still no awareness of document layout
ParagraphCheap, respects the author's own blank-line breaksBlind to headings — isolates them as orphan one-line blocks
StructuralKeeps a heading together with the content it actually introducesNeeds real markup to work from; a plain-text document with no headings gives it nothing to key on
Recursive (Concept 02)Cheapest of all four, no linguistic tooling requiredOnly recognizes separator characters — never a real sentence, paragraph, or heading

Decision framework: default to structural chunking when the source format has real markup (Markdown, HTML, well-tagged PDF). Fall back to sentence or paragraph splitting for plain text with no such structure. Recursive chunking remains the cheap fallback of last resort when no linguistic tooling is available at all.

16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

MISSING ABBREVIATION

An unlisted abbreviation causes a false sentence split

Any abbreviation absent from the exception set behaves exactly like "Dr." would without the check — a real sentence gets cut in half at a period that was never a sentence's end.

FALSE HEADING MATCH

A stray "#" gets misread as a Markdown heading

A code block or a comment containing a literal "#" at the start of a line can be wrongly detected as a heading, opening a spurious structural section.

OCR-MANGLED PERIODS

Noisy scans silently degrade sentence segmentation

A misread period from Module 06 Concept 04's OCR pipeline (tracked there by character error rate) can create or destroy a sentence boundary with no warning at all.

CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
Sentence-aware splitting always beats recursive splittingFalse in general — on already clean, short-lined prose, recursive can coincidentally align with sentence boundaries at lower implementation cost.
Paragraph splitting is a form of structural chunkingFalse — Section 07's figure proves they produce different counts (4 vs. 2) on the identical text; paragraph splitting has no concept of a heading's scope at all.

18
REALITY

Production implications

  • Abbreviation lists are language-specific — a splitter tuned for English abbreviations gains nothing on another language's conventions, a multilingual complexity named forward here and not covered further.
  • Structural parsers have to tolerate malformed Markdown or HTML gracefully — a stray "#" or an unclosed tag shouldn't be allowed to silently corrupt every section boundary after it.
  • Running a full spaCy pipeline versus a lightweight Punkt-style model is a genuine latency and cost trade-off once sentence segmentation runs at ingestion scale over an entire corpus.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Why doesn't "Dr. Smith" break under abbreviation-aware segmentation?
Before trusting the period after "Dr" as a sentence boundary, the check looks at the word immediately before it, finds "Dr" in the abbreviation exception set, and skips that period entirely — the sentence stays whole until the next real sentence-ending period.
Why does paragraph-lens isolate "## Premium Plan Exception" as its own block?
Paragraph splitting only knows about blank lines. A heading surrounded by blank lines on both sides is, by that definition, its own paragraph — the splitter has no way to know it's a heading at all, let alone that it introduces the block after it.
Why isn't structural chunking the same thing as semantic chunking?
Structural chunking follows markup it can see directly — a heading tag, a nesting level. It never inspects what the section is actually about. Grouping by meaning instead of markup is Concept 04's distinct, later mechanism.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
The passage has 4 literal periods (two inside "p.m.", one after "Mr", one after "report"), so naive period-splitting produces 4 fragments: "The meeting is at 3 p.", "m.", "Mr.", "Lee will present the Q3 report." The passage actually contains 2 real sentences: "The meeting is at 3 p.m." and "Mr. Lee will present the Q3 report." — so naive splitting introduces 2 false splits here, not 1. This one is genuinely harder than "Dr. Smith": simply adding "p" and "m" to the exception list to protect "p.m." backfires — it would also suppress the period that correctly ends the first sentence, merging both sentences into one. The abbreviation happens to fall right at a sentence's end, which a flat exception list can't tell apart from an abbreviation falling mid-sentence. That's exactly the case Punkt's statistical model and spaCy's parse-based segmenter are built to handle, and a from-scratch exception list is not.

Teach-back: explain why a bigger exception list alone cannot fully fix the "p.m." case, and what kind of signal a real sentence segmenter would need instead.

CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: why do abbreviation lists matter for sentence segmentation?
Without checking a candidate period against likely abbreviations, ordinary text containing "Dr.", "e.g.", or "U.S." produces sentence fragments that split mid-thought, degrading both embedding quality and retrieval relevance for those chunks.
Mathematical/algorithmic: derive the false-split count formula.
false_split_count = periods_total − true_sentence_count. Every literal period is a candidate boundary; every one rejected by the abbreviation check reduces the eventual sentence count by one relative to naive splitting, so the gap between the two counts is exactly the number of false splits naive splitting would have introduced.
Coding: implement the heading-nesting grouper for structural chunking.
Split on blank lines into blocks. Walk the blocks in order, keeping a current open section; whenever a block matches a heading pattern, close the current section (if any) and start a new one from that heading; otherwise append the block to whichever section is currently open. Close and emit the final open section after the loop ends.
Debugging: a structural splitter is over-splitting a document full of code samples. What do you check?
Whether a literal "#" character inside a code block or comment is being matched by the same heading-detection regex used for real Markdown headings — code fences need to be excluded from heading detection, not treated as prose.
System design: how do you choose sentence, paragraph, or structural chunking for a new corpus?
Check the source format first. Real markup with clear headings favors structural chunking. Plain text with clean paragraph breaks favors paragraph or sentence chunking. Abbreviation-dense or OCR-noisy text pushes toward a statistical sentence segmenter (Punkt, spaCy) over a hand-maintained exception list.
