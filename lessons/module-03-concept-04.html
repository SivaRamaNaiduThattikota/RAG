Hallucination reduction, grounding,
and provenance

Concepts 01-03 proved WHAT RAG is and WHY external knowledge is structurally required — a plain LLM has zero training terms for anything past its cutoff, anything private, or anything that's since drifted. This concept answers the next question: once you actually wire retrieval in, what does it buy you, mechanically? Two new terms, introduced together because they're two sides of the same payoff. Grounding means constraining generation to condition on retrieved evidence text placed literally in the prompt. Provenance means surfacing which specific retrieved passage backed a given claim — a [DocN] tag, say — so a human can check it. The model can still misread or ignore the evidence sitting right in front of it.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

Grounding and provenance, defined together — and the same correction, immediately

Two new terms this concept needs, best introduced side by side rather than one at a time:

GROUNDING

Constraining generation to condition on retrieved evidence text placed literally in the prompt. The model isn't just recalling from frozen weights anymore — it has real evidence sitting in its input.

PROVENANCE

Surfacing which specific retrieved passage backed a given claim — a tag like [Doc2] pointing to the exact chunk used — so a human can check the claim against the source.

State the correction in the same breath, because it's the part beginners skip: grounding reduces hallucination. It does not eliminate it. The evidence can be sitting right there in the prompt and the model can still misread it, skim past the relevant line, or override it with whatever it "remembers" instead.

First memory: grounding = evidence physically in the prompt; provenance = a pointer from claim to source; both help, neither is a guarantee.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Concepts 01-03 said WHY. This section says "so what does it buy you?"

Concepts 01-03 proved WHAT RAG is and WHY external knowledge is structurally required — parametric memory has zero training terms for anything past cutoff, anything private, or anything frequently-changing. That's necessity. This section is the payoff: once you actually wire retrieval in, what changes mechanically? Two things:

PAYOFF 1 · GROUNDING

The model now has real evidence text in context to condition on, instead of relying only on frozen weights that may never have held the fact, or held a stale version of it.

PAYOFF 2 · PROVENANCE

A reviewer can check a specific claim against a specific passage instead of trusting the model's unaided say-so — the citation is a handle for verification, not just a decoration.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

The Oracle RAG password-reset query, two ways

Same question, asked of the same Oracle RAG assistant, with retrieval switched off and then on:

(A) · UNGROUNDED

"What's our password-reset policy?" The model answers fluently from parametric memory — possibly confidently wrong, possibly just outdated — and there's no way to check it. It's a claim with no handle to pull on.

(B) · GROUNDED + CITED

Same question. The retriever surfaces the actual policy chunk ("passwords must be reset every 90 days"), it gets injected into the prompt, and the model's answer is conditioned on that text: "Every 90 days. [Doc2]." A human can click through [Doc2] and verify the answer against the real policy document.

Same surface shape — a short factual answer — but only one of them comes with something to check.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

Evidence as literal tokens, a citation as a forced tag

Grounding, precisely: the retrieved passage's text becomes literal tokens inside the transformer's input context, alongside the question. Generation is now conditioned on P(answer | question, retrieved_evidence) instead of just P(answer | question). That shifts probability mass toward evidence-consistent tokens — but "shifts toward" is a bias, not a hard guarantee. Module 02 Concept 10's attention dilution and lost-in-the-middle effect is one reason that shift can fail even with the right evidence sitting in context (name-checked here, not re-taught).

Provenance's mechanism is different: it's a separate structured-generation step — Module 02 Concept 11's mask, cited here, not re-derived — that forces a citation tag like [DocN] into the output, tied to which retrieved chunk was actually used.

05 · ARCHITECTURE / FLOW

Two payoffs, two exact places they attach

Reuse Concept 02's Documents-to-Answer skeleton and slot both payoffs into it:

Documents↓ Parsing↓ Chunks↓ Embedding↓ Vectors↓ Retrieval↓ Context↓ LLM↓ Answer

Retrieved evidence lands in the Context stage — that's grounding. The citation-tag mask applies during the LLM stage's decode step — that's provenance. The Answer now carries an attached source pointer. This shows where grounding and provenance sit in the pipeline — not the full offline/online mechanics of how the evidence was retrieved in the first place (Concept 06's job), and not the failure chain for when retrieval itself misses (Module 03 Concept 11's job).

07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"RAG eliminates hallucination."

False. Grounding lowers the rate — it does not zero it out. The model can still misread or ignore evidence that's sitting right in the prompt.

"If there's a citation, the claim must be true."

False. A citation shows what the model used, not that it used it correctly. A tag can point to a real, retrieved passage that doesn't actually back the specific number or claim in the answer.

"A retrieval miss and a generation misread are the same failure."

No — they're mechanically distinct. This is only named here as future material; telling them apart in practice is Module 03 Concept 11's job, not this concept's.

04
INTUITION

The same handcuffed exam-taker, now handed the textbook page

Extend Concept 02's handcuffed closed-book exam-taker analogy with two more details. The student is now handed the actual textbook page relevant to the question — that's grounding. And the student is asked to write down which page they used — that's provenance. This helps, plainly. But a student can still misread the page, skim past the one relevant line, or stubbornly write down what they remembered instead of what the page in front of them actually says. Handing over the right page doesn't force the student to read it correctly.

Analogy boundaryUnlike the student, the model has no self-awareness that it's ignoring the page in front of it. A student might eventually notice they're contradicting their own source and double-check. This is structural for the model, not a lapse in attention it could notice and correct on its own — Module 02 Concept 10's attention dilution is a mechanical account of exactly this kind of failure, not a metaphor for it.

06
MECHANISM

Seven steps from query to a checkable answer

  1. A query arrives.
  2. The retriever returns the top-k passages. (Retrieval mechanics themselves are forward-referenced to Concept 06 — not detailed here.)
  3. The passages are inserted as literal, tagged tokens into the prompt's context region.
  4. A forward pass computes logits attending over both the query tokens and the evidence tokens.
  5. The decode loop generates the answer text.
  6. A citation-tag mask — Module 02 Concept 11, cited, not rederived — forces a [DocN] tag tied to the passage index actually used.
  7. The result is an answer plus a checkable pointer, not just an answer.
07
VISUALIZATION

Two traces, side by side

Ungrounded traceprompt → logits → decode → answer. No evidence injected, no tag forced. Concept 02's plain closed loop, unchanged.
Grounded + cited traceprompt + evidence → logits (attending over both) → decode → [DocN]-masked answer. Two components added on top of the plain loop.
  1. The evidence-injection point: where retrieved text becomes part of the input tokens, before the forward pass ever runs — this is grounding.
  2. The citation-mask point: where Module 02 Concept 11's mask forces a tag during decode — this is provenance.
  3. Everything else is identical: both traces share Concept 02's same closed LLM loop underneath.

09
MATHEMATICS

A soft shift, not a hard zero-out

Define the ungrounded and grounded distributions:

P(a | q) — ungrounded: conditioned on the query alone
P(a | q, E) — grounded: E = concatenated retrieved evidence text

Grounding shifts probability mass toward tokens consistent with E — but this is a soft re-weighting via attention over the evidence tokens, not a hard zeroing-out of the alternatives. Contrast explicitly with Module 02 Concept 11's mask, which sets z'_i = -∞ for disallowed tokens — a hard constraint on the citation tag's shape. Nothing analogous hard-constrains the claim's content.

Introduce two informal hallucination-rate variables:

H_grounded < H_ungrounded, but H_grounded > 0 always

That inequality is the formal version of "reduces, not eliminates." Grounding moves the rate down. It never drives it to zero.

WORKED EXAMPLE

Three runs, one query, one underlying evidence variable

The same password-reset query, run three ways, holding the actual policy fixed at "reset every 90 days":

(1) · UNGROUNDED

No evidence in context. The model states an out-of-date policy detail — "every 30 days" — with no way for anyone to check it against anything.

(2) · GROUNDED-CORRECT

The retrieved chunk says "reset every 90 days." The model's answer matches it exactly, and a citation tag [Doc2] points to that exact chunk. Grounding worked as intended.

(3) · GROUNDED-MISREAD

The identical chunk is present in context — same "every 90 days" text, same retrieval outcome as run (2). The model's answer says "every 30 days" anyway. Grounding present, citation tag present, still wrong.

Walk through what changed between each run: only the model's behavior, never the underlying evidence. Runs (2) and (3) had access to the exact same passage. One used it correctly. One didn't — and still shipped a citation tag as if it had.

CODE

Prompt assembly and citation extraction, from scratch

PYTHON · FROM-SCRATCH PSEUDOCODE 
import re


def assemble_grounded_prompt(query, retrieved_chunks):
    # retrieved_chunks: a list of chunk strings, already ranked by
    # the retriever (retrieval mechanics themselves are Concept 06's
    # job -- this function just assembles what it's handed).
    #
    # Each chunk gets a [DocN] marker so the model has something
    # concrete to cite back to later. 1-indexed to match this
    # course's [Doc1], [Doc2], ... convention throughout.
    evidence_block = "\n".join(
        f"[Doc{i + 1}] {chunk}"
        for i, chunk in enumerate(retrieved_chunks)
    )

    return (
        f"Evidence:\n{evidence_block}\n\n"
        f"Question: {query}\n"
        f"Answer using only the evidence above, and cite the "
        f"[DocN] tag you relied on."
    )


def extract_citation(answer_text):
    # Pulls a [DocN] tag out of generated text and returns which
    # chunk index it points to -- or None when no tag is present.
    # The ungrounded case (b03/s10 run 1) is exactly this None
    # branch: no evidence was given, so nothing forced a tag, and
    # there's nothing here to extract.
    #
    # Note: this is plumbing around the citation mechanism, not the
    # mechanism itself. The mask that forces the tag's exact shape
    # at decode time is Module 02 Concept 11's code -- cited, not
    # reimplemented. This function only parses what that mask
    # already produced.
    match = re.search(r"\[Doc(\d+)\]", answer_text)
    if match is None:
        return None
    return int(match.group(1))


# the three s10 runs, illustrated
grounded_prompt = assemble_grounded_prompt(
    "What's our password-reset policy?",
    ["Onboarding checklist v2: new hires set up SSO on day one.",
     "Company password policy (v4): passwords must be reset every 90 days."],
)
print(grounded_prompt)
# Evidence:
# [Doc1] Onboarding checklist v2: new hires set up SSO on day one.
# [Doc2] Company password policy (v4): passwords must be reset every 90 days.
# ...

print(extract_citation("Our policy requires a password reset every 90 days. [Doc2]"))
# 2 -- run (2), grounded-correct

print(extract_citation("Our policy requires a password reset every 30 days. [Doc2]"))
# 2 -- run (3), grounded-misread: note the tag extracts cleanly even
# though the claim it's attached to is wrong. extract_citation can
# only tell you a tag was present and which chunk it names -- never
# whether the claim actually matches that chunk's content.

print(extract_citation("Our policy requires a password reset every 30 days."))
# None -- run (1), ungrounded: no tag was ever forced, so there's
# nothing to extract.

Notice what extract_citation deliberately can't do: tell you whether the cited chunk actually supports the claim. That check is a different function entirely, belonging to faithfulness evaluation (Module 17/18, forward-referenced) — not this concept's plumbing.

13
ECOSYSTEM

Three shapes of provenance, in production and in the literature

Concrete production mechanisms exist at different levels of rigor. LangChain's source-document return metadata — the pattern behind chains like RetrievalQAWithSourcesChain — is a plumbing-level provenance mechanism: it returns which documents were retrieved alongside the answer. Underneath any citation tag, a vector store's metadata fields — doc_id, chunk_id — are what the tag ultimately resolves to. Shuster, Poff, Chen, Kiela & Weston (2021) supply the empirical claim this whole concept leans on: retrieval augmentation measurably reduces hallucination in conversational models, though it does not remove it. Menick et al. (2022), GopherCite, goes further than this course's [DocN] tag — it produces verified quotes, a stronger provenance mechanism at real engineering cost. Gao et al. (2022), RARR, takes a different route entirely: retrieve-and-revise, an alternate post-hoc attribution approach that checks and edits a claim after generation rather than citing at generation time.

DIAGNOSIS

Four ways grounding and provenance can still go wrong

SILENT OVERRIDE

The model ignores present evidence anyway

The evidence is right there in context, but the model answers from parametric memory as if it weren't. Ties to Module 02 Concept 10's attention dilution — cited, not re-taught.

CITATION-CONTENT MISMATCH

Tag real, chunk real, claim still wrong

The tag points to a real, retrieved chunk. That chunk just doesn't actually support the specific claim made — s10 and s11's grounded-misread case.

ORPHAN CITATION

A tag pointing to a doc index never actually retrieved

A pure generation bug, not a retrieval bug — the citation names a passage that was never part of this query's evidence pool at all.

FALSE CONFIDENCE FROM PRESENCE

Reviewers stop checking once something looks cited

A tag being present can quietly lower the amount of scrutiny a claim gets, even when the tag itself is wrong — the opposite of what provenance is supposed to buy.

REALITY

Production implications, at introductory depth

  • Logging which citations were shown to users creates an audit trail — but not a correctness guarantee on its own.
  • Citation UI (clickable source links) is a product-layer decision built on top of the raw [DocN] tag, not part of the tag mechanism itself.
  • The cost of verifying citations — human review, or an automated faithfulness checker (forward-referenced to Module 17/18) — scales with how much you trust an unverified tag.
  • Legal and compliance requirements (forward-referenced to Module 20) may mandate provenance regardless of whether it changes the underlying hallucination rate.

23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Conceptual: explain grounding to a stakeholder who believes RAG is a complete hallucination fix.
Grounding means the model has the real evidence text in front of it when it answers, instead of relying only on what it memorized during training. That measurably lowers how often it makes things up. It doesn't remove the possibility entirely — the model can still misread or override the evidence it was given, so grounded answers still need spot-checking, especially for anything high-stakes.
Mathematical: why does conditioning on E shift probability mass without hard-constraining it?
Conditioning on evidence works through attention over the evidence tokens during the forward pass — it's a soft re-weighting of which tokens look likely next, not a rule that forbids other tokens outright. Contrast with Module 02 Concept 11's citation-tag mask, which does hard-constrain: it sets disallowed tokens' logits to negative infinity. That hard constraint only shapes the citation tag's syntax, though — nothing analogous forces the claim's content to match the evidence.
Debugging: given a wrong grounded answer that still carries a citation, how do you determine whether it's a retrieval miss or a generation misread?
Check whether the cited chunk actually contains information relevant to the claim. If it does and the model's claim still contradicts it, that's a generation misread. If the cited chunk (or the whole retrieved set) never contained the right information in the first place, that's a retrieval miss. Distinguishing these rigorously in practice is Module 03 Concept 11's fuller job — previewed only here.
System design: how would you build an automated check that a citation tag's passage actually supports its claim?
At a sketch level: extract the cited chunk and the claim, then run a separate faithfulness-checking pass — comparing claim against source text — and flag mismatches for review rather than trusting the tag at face value. This is forward-referenced to faithfulness evaluation (Module 18), not built out here.
