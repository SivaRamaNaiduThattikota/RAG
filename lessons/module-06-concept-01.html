MODULE 06 · CONCEPT 01

Before you parse anything,
know what you're parsing — and who to believe.

Module 05 closed Phase 1 by giving retrieval a vocabulary and its first mechanisms. Module 06 opens Phase 2 — building the knowledge layer — at a different, earlier point in the pipeline: before a single document is parsed, chunked, or embedded, a real system has to know where its knowledge actually lives. A source inventory is the catalog of every place that counts — wikis, ticket systems, shared drives, databases, PDFs sitting in an email attachment. And because different sources routinely disagree about the same fact, an ingestion pipeline needs an authority rule: when two sources conflict, which one is trusted, and why. No parsing, cleaning, or embedding happens yet — this concept only names the raw material and the trust rule everything downstream will depend on.

01 · CONCEPT FIRST

A list of places, and a rule for whose facts win

SOURCE INVENTORY

The catalog of every place knowledge actually lives — a wiki, a ticket system, a shared drive full of PDFs, a database table, a Slack channel — before any of it has been touched.

AUTHORITY

When two sources state the same fact differently, authority is the answer to "which one do we trust, and why" — a policy decision, not a coin flip.

First memory: you cannot ingest what you haven't inventoried, and you cannot trust an ingested fact without a rule for what wins when sources disagree.
02 · WHY IT EXISTS

Real knowledge is scattered, and it contradicts itself

A toy RAG demo often assumes one clean folder of text files. A real organization's knowledge is spread across a wiki, a ticketing system, a code repository, spreadsheets on someone's laptop, and PDFs attached to three-year-old emails — and several of those places disagree about the same fact, because one was updated and the others weren't. If you skip the inventory step, you silently ingest whatever's easiest to reach and miss whatever isn't. If you skip authority, your system will confidently answer a question using a stale Slack message when a current policy PDF says something else — and it will have no way of knowing that's what happened.

03 · CONCRETE EXAMPLE

One fact, three sources, one contradiction

A new hire asks the Oracle-backed assistant: "how many vacation days do I get?" Three places in the organization mention it:

SLACK MESSAGE, 3 DAYS OLD

A teammate writes, "I think it's 18 days now?" — informal, uncertain, not an authored policy.

WIKI PAGE, 14 MONTHS OLD

"New hires receive 15 vacation days per year." — written once, never revisited.

HR POLICY PDF, v4, 2 MONTHS OLD

"New hires accrue 18 vacation days per year, effective this version." — the official, current, versioned policy document.

All three get ingested if the source inventory includes Slack, the wiki, and the HR document store. Only an authority rule decides that the answer given to the new hire should come from the HR PDF, not the wiki or the Slack message.

04 · TECHNICAL MECHANISM

What a source inventory records, and what authority resolves

SOURCE INVENTORY RECORDS

For each source: its location, its type (wiki, ticket system, database, file share, API), who owns it, how often it changes, and how to reach it — nothing about its content yet.

AUTHORITY RESOLVES

Given two or more sources that state the same fact differently, which one the system should prefer, using a rule fixed in advance — not decided fact-by-fact at query time.

WHAT'S DEFERRED

Actually opening and parsing each source's content (Concepts 02-04), cleaning it (05), deduplicating repeated facts (06), tracking versions (07), and recording provenance (08) all come later in this module.

06 · IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS

Three pairs beginners routinely blur

TermIs NOTWhy it matters
Source inventoryParsingThe inventory says a PDF exists and where; parsing (Concepts 02-04) opens it and extracts text — the inventory step can be complete while zero files have been read.
AuthorityJust picking the newest oneRecency is one input to authority, not the whole rule — an outdated official policy PDF can still outrank a fresh but informal Slack guess, depending on the policy.
Cataloging sourcesDeduplicationCataloging lists distinct places knowledge lives; dedup (Concept 06) removes repeated or near-identical content once it's already been pulled from those places.
07 · COMMON MISTAKES

What beginners often assume, and shouldn't

"We'll just ingest whatever we can easily find."

Easy-to-reach sources (a shared drive) and authoritative sources (a versioned policy system) aren't always the same places — skipping a deliberate inventory means the system's knowledge is shaped by convenience, not by what actually matters.

"Newer always means more trustworthy."

A recent but off-the-cuff Slack message isn't automatically more trustworthy than an older, official document — authority depends on the source's type and role, with recency as one factor among several.

"Authority is decided per answer, at query time."

Deciding trust fact-by-fact, on the fly, is inconsistent and unauditable — a real system fixes an authority policy in advance, so the same conflict resolves the same way every time.

"This is the same problem as deduplication."

Dedup asks "are these two pieces of content the same thing said twice?" Authority asks "these two pieces of content disagree about a fact — which do I believe?" — related, but not the same question, and Concept 06 handles the first.

08 · YOUR PROJECT MAPPING

Naming the Oracle RAG project's sources and authority rule

SOURCE INVENTORYEvery place the Oracle-backed assistant is allowed to pull from

Its own tables, an internal wiki, a ticket system, and any policy documents — named and scoped before ingestion begins.

AUTHORITY RULEOfficial documents and the database outrank chat and informal notes

A fixed policy: structured database rows and versioned official documents win over tickets, wikis, and chat, with recency breaking ties within the same source type.

09 · WHAT YOU SHOULD UNDERSTAND

Readiness check before moving on

Two definitions

A source inventory (the catalog of every place knowledge lives) and authority (a fixed rule for which source wins when facts disagree).

Inventory before parsing

Cataloging a source is complete before any of its content is opened or extracted — that's Concepts 02-04's job.

Authority is a policy, not a vibe

Fixed in advance, based on source type and role, with recency as a tiebreaker — not decided per query.

Nothing cleaned or deduped yet

Authority resolves disagreement between sources; it doesn't remove duplicate or near-duplicate content, which is Concept 06's job.

04
INTUITION

A librarian's shelving list, before a single book is read

Imagine a librarian tasked with building a reference desk. Before reading a single book, they first need a list of every room, cabinet, and box that might contain something worth shelving — the archive room, the donated-boxes closet, the digital catalog, the pamphlet drawer. That list is the source inventory. Now imagine two donated encyclopedias disagree about a fact: one says a bridge opened in 1932, the other in 1936. The librarian needs a standing rule — perhaps "prefer the more recently published, professionally edited source over the older, self-published one" — rather than flipping a coin each time a patron asks. That standing rule is authority.

Analogy boundaryThe librarian's shelving list only names the boxes; it says nothing about what's readable inside them. A real ingestion pipeline hits exactly the same gap — cataloging a file share doesn't tell you whether the files inside are scanned images, clean text, or corrupted exports, which is precisely why parsing needs its own concept next.

06
MECHANISM

From scattered knowledge to a resolvable conflict

Knowledge exists across many systems — wikis, tickets, databases, file shares, chat — before anyone has organized them for ingestion.
A source inventory is built — each system is named, typed, and scoped as in or out of what the pipeline will draw from.
Later concepts pull content from each source — parsing (02-04), cleaning (05), dedup (06), all out of this concept's scope.
Two sources are found to disagree about a fact — the trigger for authority to matter at all.
auth(s) resolves the conflict — the higher-ranked source's claim is used; the loss is recorded, not silently discarded (a forward reference to Concept 08's provenance).
07
VISUALIZATION

The authority ranking as an ordered ladder

Rank 1 · highestLive system of record, current official policy document
Rank 2 · middleWiki page, email thread — authored but not continuously maintained
Rank 3 · lowestChat message, informal note — fast, but unauthored and unversioned

The ladder is the rule Section 10 and the lab both apply: rank by source type first; only break ties within the same rank using recency. A rank-1 source that's slightly older still beats a rank-3 source that's brand new.

09
MATHEMATICS

A ranking as a total order, deliberately simple

Authority as used here is a total order over source types, with recency as an explicit tiebreaker — no probabilities or learned weights yet:

auth(s) = (type_rank(s), -recency(s)) prefer s over s' if auth(s) < auth(s') (lexicographic: type first, then more-recent wins)

type_rank groups sources into tiers (e.g. 1 = system of record or current official document, 2 = authored-but-static wiki or email, 3 = informal chat); recency is compared only within the same tier. This concept deliberately stops at a fixed, hand-set ranking — Module 20's governance material is where confidence scores and per-tenant overrides would extend it, out of scope here.

10
WORKED EXAMPLE

Resolving b03's vacation-days conflict by the rule

Applying auth(s) = (type_rank, -recency) to the three sources from b03:

SourceTypetype_rankAgeauth(s)
HR policy PDF, v4Current official policy document12 months(1, -2mo) — wins
Wiki pageAuthored, not continuously maintained214 months(2, -14mo)
Slack messageInformal chat33 days(3, -3d) — loses despite being newest

The Slack message has the best recency of all three, and still loses, because type_rank is compared first: rank 1 beats rank 2 and rank 3 regardless of age. That's the entire point of stating the rule as lexicographic rather than "just pick the newest" — it's the exact mistake named in Section 17.

11
CONCRETE USE

Four cases: two clean fits, a boundary case, a mistake

CLEAN FIT

Corporate policy assistant

Inventory: HR system, wiki, ticket archive, Slack. Authority: current official documents and the HR database outrank wiki and chat, unambiguously.

CLEAN FIT

Engineering on-call assistant

Inventory: a live scheduling API, a team wiki, email threads. Authority: the live system of record always outranks the wiki, even though both are "official-sounding."

BOUNDARY CASE

Two sources of the same rank, same age

If two rank-1 sources genuinely disagree with no recency difference, the authority rule as stated doesn't resolve it — this is a real gap that forces either a manual override or a stricter ranking, not a flaw to paper over silently.

MISTAKE TO AVOID

Ranking by how confident the text sounds

A Slack message that says "I'm 100% sure it's 18 days" is not more authoritative for sounding certain — authority is a property of the source's type and role, never of the claim's wording or tone.

12
CODE

Modeling sources and applying the authority rule — no parsing yet

This concept's code represents an inventory and applies the auth(s) ranking to a conflict; it does not open, parse, or extract text from any real file — that begins at Concept 02.

PYTHON · SOURCE INVENTORY AND AUTHORITY RESOLUTION, NO PARSING 
from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Source:
    name: str
    type_rank: int      # 1 = system of record / current official doc, 2 = authored-static, 3 = informal
    age_days: int        # smaller is more recent
    claim: str

def authority_key(s: Source):
    # Lexicographic: type_rank ascending first, then more-recent (smaller age_days) wins.
    return (s.type_rank, s.age_days)

sources = [
    Source("HR policy PDF v4", type_rank=1, age_days=60,  claim="18 days"),
    Source("Wiki page",        type_rank=2, age_days=420, claim="15 days"),
    Source("Slack message",    type_rank=3, age_days=3,   claim="18 days (uncertain)"),
]

winner = min(sources, key=authority_key)
assert winner.name == "HR policy PDF v4"          # rank 1 wins despite not being newest
assert winner.claim == "18 days"

# The Slack message is newest by far but still loses -- type_rank is compared first.
newest = min(sources, key=lambda s: s.age_days)
assert newest.name == "Slack message"
assert newest.name != winner.name
13
ECOSYSTEM

The same two ideas, inside real ingestion tooling

Enterprise data-catalog tools (data governance platforms, source registries) exist specifically to maintain a source inventory at scale — recording ownership, sensitivity, and refresh cadence for every connected system, the same fields Section 05's meta(s) names generically. Connector frameworks used by RAG tooling (LangChain document loaders, LlamaIndex readers) each correspond to one inventoried source type; authority itself is rarely built into these frameworks directly and is usually a deliberate design decision layered on top, exactly the gap this concept is naming before Module 20 revisits it as governance.

CONTRAST

Fixed authority ranking vs. confidence-scored trust

ApproachWhat it capturesWhen it's enough
Fixed authority ranking (this concept)A hand-set, auditable order of source types with recency as tiebreakerMost ingestion pipelines — simple, explainable, and enough to resolve the common cases
Confidence-scored trustA learned or continuously updated score per source, blending recency, corroboration, and historical accuracyLarge-scale, high-stakes systems where a fixed ranking proves too coarse — a governance-layer extension, not built in this module
16
DIAGNOSIS

Failure modes

MISSING SOURCE

A real knowledge location was never inventoried

No authority rule can rescue a fact that was never ingested because its source wasn't on the list — an inventory-completeness problem, not an authority problem.

UNDEFINED RANK

A new source type shows up with no assigned rank

Without a rank, auth(s) can't compare it to anything — a real pipeline needs a default rank and a process for classifying unfamiliar sources, or conflicts involving it silently misresolve.

SILENT OVERRIDE

A losing source's claim is discarded with no record

If the loser of a conflict isn't logged anywhere, nobody can audit why the system answered one way instead of another — Concept 08's provenance concept exists specifically to prevent this.

17
CORRECTION

Misconceptions

Incorrect beliefCorrection
The newest source is always the most authoritativeFalse — Section 10's worked example: the newest source (Slack) loses to an older, higher-type-rank source (the HR PDF).
A confident-sounding claim is a more trustworthy claimFalse — Section 11's mistake case: tone or certainty in the text has nothing to do with the source's structural authority.
Cataloging sources and parsing their content are the same stepFalse — Section 06's mechanism: the inventory can be complete while zero files have been opened; parsing is Concept 02 onward.
18
REALITY

Production implications

  • An incomplete source inventory is invisible until someone asks a question the system can't answer well — inventory gaps surface as answer-quality gaps much later, making the connection hard to trace back.
  • Authority rankings need an owner and a review process; treating them as "set once, forget forever" lets stale rankings quietly misresolve new kinds of conflicts as an organization's tools change.
  • Some organizations must retain a record of which source lost a conflict and why, for compliance or dispute-resolution reasons — a forward reference to Concept 08's provenance and lineage tracking.
19
RECALL

Knowledge check

Is authority decided per query, or fixed in advance as a policy?
Fixed in advance — Section 05 defines auth(s) as a standing ranking function over sources, applied consistently, not re-decided each time a question is asked.
In auth(s) = (type_rank, -recency), which factor is compared first?
type_rank — recency only breaks ties between sources that already share the same type_rank, which is exactly why the newest source can still lose in Section 10.
What's the difference between a source inventory being complete and a source being fully parsed?
A complete inventory only means every relevant source has been identified and cataloged; parsing (Concept 02 onward) is the separate step of actually opening a source and extracting usable content from it.

FEEDBACK

Solution and teach-back

Open the worked solution
Live inventory database — type_rank 1, it is a system of record updated continuously. Handbook PDF — type_rank 1 or a close 2, it is official and versioned but only quarterly, so it's less current than a live system for fast-changing facts. Support ticket system — type_rank 2, authored records but not the source of truth for stock or policy facts themselves. Unowned spreadsheet — type_rank 3, informal and unmaintained despite looking structured. 23
CAREER PRACTICE

Interview questions

Define a source inventory without using the word "list."
A deliberately scoped catalog of every distinct system or repository an ingestion pipeline is authorized to draw knowledge from, recorded before any of that content is opened or extracted.
Why isn't "always trust the newest source" a sufficient authority policy?
Because recency says nothing about a source's type, ownership, or role — an informal, unauthored source can be the newest and still be the least trustworthy, as shown by the Slack-versus-HR-PDF conflict.
A stakeholder asks why your ingestion pipeline needs an authority rule at all if every source is "generally accurate." What's your answer?
Because "generally accurate" sources still disagree with each other on specific facts, and without a standing rule the system's answer to the same conflict becomes inconsistent and unauditable — exactly the failure mode in Section 16's silent-override case.
Design question: how would you extend this concept's fixed ranking if two sources of identical type_rank and identical age still disagreed?
The stated rule as given doesn't resolve it (Section 11's boundary case) — a real extension needs either a manual override process, an additional tiebreaker field, or escalation to a human reviewer, which is the kind of governance extension Module 20 covers.
